# Chapter 1 - Overview & Typology
## Typological Overview
Lise is a fully predicative, nounless, prosody-heavy language whose grammar is primarily carried by lexical specificity, predicate chaining, and constrained word order rather than inflectional morphology or nominal syntax. It is specialized to the ecology of [[environment-east-floodplains]] and is an oral-only language.

Note that since the language has no written form, its sounds and words will be represented here by characters from the roman alphabet.
# Chapter 2 - Phonology & Phonotactics
**Note on written language:** Lise in A0 did not yet have a written form, therefore, in this grammar, we will be using roman alphabet approximations to represent the spoken language.
## Phonology
The phonology of Lise is represented by the following characters:
- l - regular l like in English "lick"
- r - heavily trilled Spanish-style r "guerrillo"
- b - voiced bilabial as in English "boat"
- p - voiceless bilabial as in English "octopus"
- f - voiceless labiodental fricative as in English "friend"
- v - voiced labiodental fricative as in English "victory"
- d - voiced alveolar plosive as in English "dig"
- t - voiceless alveolar plosive as in English "tip"
- s - voiceless postalveolar fricative like English "share"
- w - voiced labial-velar approximant like English "owing"
- j - voiced palatal approximant like English "young"
- n - voiced uvular nasal like English "nope"

**Vowels**
- a - open-back unrounded vowel, like English "pause"
- o - close-mid back rounded vowel, closest in English is "go" (but no dipthong)
- u - close back rounded vowel, like French "vous"
- i - close front unrounded vowel, like English "seat"
- e - open-mid front unrounded vowel, like English "set"
- ö - close-mid front rounded vowel like German "schön"
- ü - close front rounded vowel like German "tür"

**Note on pronunciation:** Historically, many Lise roots had "n" codas, and these were pronounced as they are written here. In Age 0, Lise speakers no longer pronounce these "n" sounds at the end of words except in certain contexts. Any "n"-final word does not have the "n" sound, but rather nasalizes the preceding vowel.
## Phonotactics
Most syllables take one of three common shapes:

- CVC
- CV
- CCV
# Chapter 3 - Prosody
## Stress & Rhythm
Default stress falls on the initial syllable of a word, and only shifts when the prosodic overlays override this. The overall sentence rhythm is terse, succinct, and efficient.
## Prosodic Overlays
Prosody is a crucial aspect to everyday Lise speech, since the predicate units are, semantically speaking, broad and relational. Prosody helps specify things like urgency, certainty, intentionality, and duration/repetition.

**A note on cooccurrence of prosody overlays:** while in theory all four axes could be activated on a single root all at once, in practice this almost never happens. Most commonly one or two of the axes are employed.

**A note on prosody notation:** Since the Lise language has no true written form, in this grammar we will use a simple notation system to indicate spoken prosody nuances. The symbols associated with each prosody change are listed in the below tables for each subsection. The notation order is as follows \[activation]\[continuity]\[control]\[epistemics].
### Activation
Activation in Lise is the degree of intensity, forcefulness, or urgency with which an event is occurring. It describes the energy of the event. A glottal interruption is a glottal stop inserted in the middle of the first vowel's pronunciation (*jon* -> *jo:on* - "go outside", *sihü* -> *si:ihü* - "), while a glottal punctuation is simply a stop at the end of a word ("dru" -> "dru:"), or, in the case of nasal vowels, a pronounced n.

| modification                  | meaning                   | notation |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------- | -------- |
| default                       | generic                   |          |
| glottal interruption          | increased force/intensity | :        |
| glottal puncuation/voiced 'n' | increased urgency         | :        |
Examples:
- *Pfi:i frawa* - on-strike (forcefully) swim (strike the fish hard)
- *Jan; terne* - in-go (urgently) cover-from-above (get into the shelter quickly)
### Continuity
Continuity is how temporally extended or repeated an event is. Duration lengthening, repetition.

**Glottal Stop At Onset**
A glottal stop at the onset of a predicate marks the perfective or bounded aspect. This is a highly productive yet non-essential aspect indicator. It is not the same as a "past tense". It simply denotes an action or event is complete or bounded. By default this indicator is **only used when ambiguity demands it**. Otherwise, perfective/imperfective aspect can be inferred from context during most conversations.

| duration type      | meaning                           | notation |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- | -------- |
| regular/short      | default/generic                   |          |
| lengthened         | temporally unbounded, extended    | <        |
| repeat word 2ce    | recurring, repeated               | ::       |
| glottal stop onset | perfective aspect, bounded action | :        |
Examples:
- *Sanran< jele* - wait-watch-for (for an extended period of time) slither-slide (wait and keep an eye out for a snake)
- *Göten::göten* - recover-breathe recover-breathe (take your time to rest and recover breathe)
- *Serö::serö* - slip-lose-footing slip-lose-footing (I keep slipping and stumbling)
- *:Lise* - communicate (bound action, completed) (I spoke with someone)
### Control
Control is how deliberate, stable, or managed the event is. Syllabic emphasis. On monosyllabic words, contrast is made for end-emphasis by stretching vowel out, with emphasis on second "half" of vowel (i.e. "jon!" vs "joON"). Otherwise beginning-emphasis places stress on initial syllable, and end-emphasis places stress on final syllable.

| emphasis                | meaning                       | notation |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------- |
| beginning               | default                       |          |
| beginning (accentuated) | emphatic controlled, agentive | \[       |
| end                     | not controlled                | \]       |
Examples:
- *\[Trato* - cut-edge (deliberate) (I cut on purpose)
- *Trato\]* - cut-edge (accidental, uncontrolled) (I cut on accident)
### Epistemic
Epistemics covers how directly known, certain, or inferred an event is. Intonation contrasts.

| pitch    | meaning                          | notation |
| -------- | -------------------------------- | -------- |
| upward   | inferred, uncertain source       | /        |
| downward | direct knowledge, certain source | \        |
Examples:
- *Fe/* - disappear-into-reeds (uncertain/I think I saw/heard it) (It disappeared into the reeds I think)
- *Fe\\* - disappear-into-reads (I saw it/I know for sure) (It definitely disappeared into the reeds)
# Chapter 4 - Predicate Structure
## Predicate Theory
In Lise, all lexical roots are fundamentally predicative. They encode events, states, affordances, or relations. There is no noun or adjective class in the language.

Predicate roots can function referentially without nominalization. Predicate meaning is interpreted through a combination of context, prosody, and discourse, so reference emerges pragmatically.

When referencing "something", they are NOT NOUNS. They are still immutably verb-like, describing an action or process, not serving as an object label. Rather, they are attentional prompts toward relational/environmental structures.

For example, *sanho* does not simply "mean tree" or "mean shade". It activates the concept of blocking light and casting a shadow. Then context determines the source, the agent, the target, and the intention.

Predicates may denote:
- characteristic behavior
- current activity
- affordance
- environmental relation
- bodily state
- perceptual event
## Predicate Families
There are a handful of predicate "families" - groups of semantically related roots that share a common base meaning, with different versions encoding different locative data. These core verbs are among some of the most important and productive, as the language has no locative particles or case endings available for describing locational relationships. The predicate families are listed out in full here.

Note that, since there is no "locationless" variant of any of these predicates, the language favors a specific variant of each family for general use. ## Stative Predication
Predicates can describe an energetic action *(Pisa haslu* - strike the thrashing thing) or a stative relationship by identifying the behavior linking things *(sanho* - shade relation exists).

Predicates functioning in a stative manner make up for the lack of a dedicated "to be"-like copula. Instead of "the child is sheltered", one simply indicates the child with a gesture and utters *sanho*.

Some predicates naturally lend themselves to stative description *(sihi* - on-rest), others more toward impactful or motion-bound action *(pfi* - on-strike), while others still can comfortably function in both spaces *(sanho* - shade-give).
# Chapter 5 - Reference & Spatial Relations
## Discourse Tracking
## Reference System
Due to a lack of a proper noun system in the language, Lise speakers refer to "things" using predicates that describe an event or process. There are three common referential strategies, as follows. Which one is selected depends on a single basic principle:

**References are selected by attentional salience.**

Not "what objectively is this thing?", but rather "what distinguishes it *right now* for us?"
### Characteristic Reference
What does it do characteristically? What enduringly defines it? This is the closest approach to general "labels" for "things" - however, the predicate describes the characteristic behavior or activity, it is not a label for the thing itself.

- *Whü sanho* - "Follow shade-give" (Follow the trees)
- *Wipon hosü* - "Track breathe" (Track the breathing-thing/animal)
### Situational Reference
What is it doing *right now?* Situational reference differs from characteristic reference in that it describes the actual current activity or behavior unfolding, which may or may not be the characteristic activity or behavior of the thing in question.

- *Pisa haslu* - "Strike (through) thrash-struggle" (Strike/pierce the struggling animal)
### Affordance Reference
What relevant function does it presently provide? Rather than describe what a thing is doing in an agentive sense, it denotes the benefit or advantage or other purpose a thing serves or can serve to the speaker or other conversation subjects.

Affordance reference is technically a subtype, as an affordance reference can be either characteristic or situational.

- *Fran swatö* - "Carry (inside) eat/nourish" (Take inside animal-for-food)
## Locative Architecture
Due to a lack of locative cases or particles, Lise relies heavily on a core set of predicate families that encode locative relationships inherently (see chapter 4 for a full list of these predicate families and their roots).

A locative predicate encodes a location relative to the final predicate that follows it in a predicate chain. Here are some examples.

- *Vran bla sanho* - inside-gather fruit-grow shade-give (Gather fruit in tree)
- *Jan: terne* - inside-go (urgently) cover-from-above (Go quickly inside the shelter)
- *Wepon lura* - outside-follow pool-waterly (Follow me out of the water)
- *Fü::fü sanho* - near-gather near-gather shade-give (We'll meet/gather by the tree multiple times/regularly)

Note that multiple predicates may follow the locative predicate; it is the final predicate in the chain that anchors the locative relationship.
# Chapter 6 - Predicate Chaining & Event Structure
## Predicate Chaining
Predicate chaining is how information more complex than a simple command can be conveyed. Generally the first predicate is the main one describing the salient action, followed by additional predicates that reference things relative to the primary predicate. In the following outline, optional predicates are marked with an asterisk (\*).

\[salient action/event]\[target reference*]\[additional target descriptors]\[locative anchor*]

Some predicate chains might have just the primary action, some might have the primary action and the locative predicate, or others still might have a primary action and a target predicate but no locative one.

The key rules can be summed thus:
- The most salient information is the head of the chain
- If present, location anchors come at the very end
- When in doubt on paper, context would heavily disambiguate in practice

## Aspect & Tense
Lise does not have tense. It has very limited but productive aspect managed by prosody. A glottal stop at the onset of a predicate indicates the event encoded is bounded or complete/perfective. This is not a "past tense" but often is used to describe things that happened in the past. See [[C4-L1-A0#Chapter 3 - Prosody]] for more information.
# Chapter 7 - Information Structure & Pragmatics
## Focus Structure
As stated in the previous chapter, in predicate chains, the most salient predicate comes at the beginning of the chain. **This order is fixed.**

Prosody can be used for emphasis, tapping into any of the four axes of prosodic expression to highlight a specific nuance of a specific word. This is useful for contrastive focus "THIS action, not that one" might be emphasized with the intensifier glottal stop interruption. See more on these strategies in [[C4-L1-A0#Chapter 3 - Prosody]].

Speakers also often use gaze, pointing, and orientation to establish focus. In the same vein, due to shared physical context between speakers, topic persistence is fairly high during discourse,, and once something is initially referenced it does not need to be repeated multiple times unless significant time has passed or another topic is discussed in the meantime.
## Discourse Strategy
How does Lise organize communication across larger stretches of interaction, such as in an extended conversation or narrative? Ambiguity is managed heavily by shared environment, shared knowledge, and visible context. All of these reduce the need for explicit repetition unless clarity demands it.

**Disambiguation**
When a reference is misinterpreted or too vague, it is common to use multiple predicate roots to describe the thing. An animal might be clarified thus:

- *Hosü, haslu, jele* - Breathe, thrash-struggle, slither-slide (we're talking about a thrashing snake or some such, something now easily identifiable in the immediate surroundings)

**Sequential Event Narration**
In sequential event narration, to keep distinct clauses perceptibly separate from each other, speakers pause ever so slightly longer between two new ideas.

- *Jan, pfi* - inside-go, on-strike (go inside, then strike; not go into thing that is striking)
- *Pfi hosü haslu, jon* - on-strike breathe thrash-struggle, outside-go (strike the struggling animal, then exit)

Since there is no tense in Lise, any time temporal sequencing would be ambiguous, the perfective prosodic marker (onset glottal stop) can be used to mark a bounded event, implying it was completed in the past. This can be used in contrast with imperfect, default predicate roots that are understood to come later in sequence.

**Shared Attentional Grounding**
One of the biggest contributing factors to clear communication is a shared attentional grounding - co-presence, shared perception, shared orientation. If two people are looking at a tree and start talking about *sanho* (shade-give), then it is very apparent they are talking about the tree doing that and not something else.
## Social Pragmatics
***Wapun***
*Wapun* is the only dedicated auxiliary predicate in Lise and plays a crucial role in socially charged interactions. It explicitly marks a persons intent, and also plays a significant role in asking for and giving consent in social exchanges.

- *Wapun nölö?* - intend mutual-physical-interact? (may I hug you/kiss you/be intimate with you?\*)
- *Wapun nölö* or simply *Wapun* - the affirmative response

\*Note that *nölö* in particular is a predicate that is highly dependent on physical context for disambiguation of its exact meaning.

In the case of socially consequential actions, explicit intent marking is required. Omission thereof *(nölö?)* would be interpreted more as an inquiry of a past occurrence rather than a future proposal. Thus, *wapun* also has a futuristic nuance to it in addition to expressing intent (although *wapun* can very much be discussed regarding past actions, especially when the perfective aspect is used).

- *Wapun jon* - intend outside-go (I intend to go outside)
- *;Wapun hehe sanho* - intend (perfective) above-climb shade-give (I meant to climb up the tree)

**Affirmation vs Negation**
Lise has no dedicated affirmative or negative particles such as "yes" and "no" - rather, affirmation is expressed by repeating the proposal with downward (epistemically certain) inflection, and refusal is indicated simply by silence or shaking of the head. Repetition of the proposal with upward inflection can be an expression similar to a hesitant "maybe" when responding to a request.

**Excessive Activation**
Excessive prosodic activation (intensity axis) may sound particularly aggressive or alarming outside of appropriate contexts, similar to someone losing their patience and yelling (it may accompany this).

**Hunting Context**
While hunting dangerous prey, or in situations where explicit communication is crucial, speakers heavily favor situational and affordance references over more abstract characteristic ones (see [[C4-L1-A0#Chapter 5 - Reference & Spatial Relations]] for a breakdown of referential strategies).
# Chapter 8 - Embodied Communication
## Gesture, Gaze, Orientation
A speaker may say *sanho* while:
- looking upward toward trees
- looking toward a shelter
- looking at a child
Each of these changes interpretation.

Gaze functions supplementarily as **referential indexing**. Pointing or gesturing can alternatively or additionally reinforce what is being referenced.

 Sometimes gaze, gesture, and/or posture plus a single predicate is a complete utterance. *Nah* (near-watch) while pointing towards reeds: "something is there / watch there".

Meaning is not contained strictly inside the spoken words. Meaning is distributed across the world (shared environmental context), the body, and speech.
## Deixis
While most deictic specification is handled by body language and shared attentional focus, locative predicate roots can be used to explicitly clarify contrast between multiple things.

- *Na* - near-watch
- *Nahö* - across-watch

This can be used in conjunction with other complete predicate chains, i.e.:

- *Pfi hosü, nahö* - on-strike breathe, across-watch (hit the animal over there)

Other locative lexical families have similar locative contrasts that can be used in the same way.
# Chapter 9 - Diachrony
## Sound Changes
### All Roots
**G -> H Shift**
- Velars erode to breathy fricative
**Unstressed medial vowel syncope**
- Weak medial vowels are syncopated when doing so creates an allowed onset cluster. Low /a/ and rounded /ö/ are most prone to loss; /i/ usually palatalizes the preceding consonant instead of disappearing. Syncope is blocked when it would create disfavored clusters or collapse common lexical families.
**Final coda simplification**
- Vn -> nasal vowel
- applies to /a/, /o/, and /e/
**Disyllabic limit**
- Lise predicate roots may not exceed 2 syllables, and longer ones reduce as necessary
### Frequency-driven Over-erosion (15-25% Roots)
**Cluster simplification**
- CCV -> CV
- exceptions: sl, shw, wh, and pretty much any Cl or Cr combo
**Lenition of voiceless stops in weak environments**
- voiceless stop + h turns into fricative
- i.e. ph -> f
- selective application
**Voicing of fricatives in /h/ cluster reduction**
- i.e. fhü -> vü
- limited, 20-30%
### Lexicalized Distortions/Analogical Remodeling (5-10% Roots)
Words that become historically odd because of:
- semantic specialization
- analogy
- taboo replacement
- ritual preservation
Example:
- johü -> jü
