# Chapter 1 - Typology
## Environmental/Force-based Lexicon
Survival-relevant terms are highly specific, with minute distinctions encoded lexically (falling lightly vs. heavily, or running quickly vs. running with great effort against resistance - such as deep snow).

Abstract and general concepts are less prominent.

Cultural implications are that the speakers are more attuned to physical states, environmental feedback, and success/failure of actions.
## Verb-centered Semantics
Verbs carry the most semantic weight - nouns are often secondary or less relevant. Whereas nouns are simply event participants, verbs are the structure of reality.

In the case of many verbs, their meanings are fine-grained and physical, with specific nuances encoded lexically. This increases the cognitive load on new learners of the language but increases semantic efficiency for fluent speakers by decreasing the need for adverbs and other modifiers in many cases.

Additionally, due to the importance of resulting states, Tsaqun verbs prioritize aspect over tense, encoding completedness, ongoingness, resultative state, and failure/success over chronological sequence.
## Ergative-Absolutive Alignment
Tsaqun is an ergative-absolutive language.

**Absolutive** is the default grammatical case.

**Ergative** is the case used for the **subject of transitive verbs**. In practice:

- **S** - sole argument of an intransitive verb
- **A** - agent of a transitive verb
- **P** - patient of a transitive verb

S and P behave the same (absolutive). A is different (ergative).

The ergative marking is **always required** on the agent of transitive verbs.

The ergative is not used in anti-passive constructs, as the verb is effectively rendered intransitive.

The early speakers of Tsaqun prioritized **outcomes** of **events** due to harsh environment where survival was the number one priority. Who hunted the food mattered less than the fact that the food was brought into the camp. The ergative case still allows them to specify agents when necessary or directly relevant to the discussion.
# Chapter 2 - Phonology
**Note on written language:** Tsaqun in A0 did not yet have a written form, therefore, in this grammar, we will be using roman alphabet approximations to represent the spoken language.
## Phoneme Inventory
See below this section for a helpful reference on linguistic terms.

Consonants:
- k - voiceless velar plosive, like English "k"
- kh - voiceless velar fricative
- q - voiceless uvular plosive, further back than English "k"
- qh - voiceless uvular fricative, like French "r" but voiceless
- g - voiced velar plosive, like English hard "g"
- gh - voiced velar fricative
- ĝ - voiced uvular plosive, further back than English hard "g" - (U-011F, U-011E)
- ĝh - voiced uvular fricative
- l - alveolar lateral approximant, like English "l"
- hl - voiceless lateral fricative, like English "l" with "h" sound simultaneously
- h -voiceless glottal fricative, like English "h"
- r - alveolar tap, like Spanish single-trilled "r"
- n - alveolar nasal, like English "n"
- t - voiceless alveolar plosive, like English "t"
- d - voiced alveolar plosive, like English "d"
- j - voiced palatal approximant, like English "y" as consonant
- w - voiced labial-velar approximant, like English "w"
- p - voiceless bilabial stop, like English "p"
- b - voiced bilabial stop, like English "b"
- m - voiced bilabial nasal, like English "m"
- s - voiceless alveolar sibilant fricative, like English "s"
- ts - voiceless alveolar sibilant affricate, like the final sound in English "ants"
Vowels:
- a - open-back unrounded vowel, like English "pause"
- o - close-mid back rounded vowel, closest in English is "go" (but no dipthong)
- u - close back rounded vowel, like French "vous"
- i - close front unrounded vowel, like English "seat"
- e - open-mid front unrounded vowel, like English "set"

**Voiced vs. Voiceless**
Illustrated cleanly with the contrast between English sounds p, k, t (voiceless), and b, g, d (voiced).

**Uvular, Velar, and Alveolar**
The uvula is the piece of flesh dangling down in the back of the mouth towards the throat. Therefore **uvular** sounds are produced by making contact between the uvula and the tongue.

Velar refers to the part of the roof of the mouth between the uvular region and the front of the mouth. In English, we use this area to make "k" and hard "g" sounds.

Alveolar is the area just behind the top front teeth on the roof of the mouth. In English we touch the tongue here to make an "s" sound or a "n" sound, for example.

**Fricatives & Plosives**
Fricatives are produced by forcing air through a narrow channel made by placing two articulators (i.e. tongue and mouth roof or uvula) close together (touching or just apart).

Plosives are consonants created by completely blocking airflow in the mouth and then releasing it suddenly, creating a small "explosion" sound. The English "k" is a plosive consonant, for example.
## Phonotactics

Tsaqun roots are predominantly mono- and disyllabic. Preferred root shapes are **CVC, CVCC, CCVC, and CVCVC**. Open syllables occur, but closed syllables are strongly favored, especially root-finally.

### Onsets

- Any single consonant may occur as an onset.
- Complex onsets are restricted.
- Productive complex onsets are primarily **obstruent + liquid**.
- **qn** is marginal and lexicalized.

### Nuclei

- Vowel nuclei are simple only.
- No diphthongs are evidenced.
- Vowel inventory: **a, e, i, o, u**.

### Codas

- Single-consonant codas are common.
- Two-consonant codas are allowed, especially **sonorant + obstruent**.
- Heavy codas are common root-finally.
- Triple codas are avoided.
- Obstruent + obstruent codas are marginal and should generally be avoided in new roots.

### General restrictions

- No vowel hiatus inside simple roots.
- No long vowels currently evidenced.
- Roots strongly favor compact, high-information forms with final closure.
## Stress System
In general, stress falls on the first syllable of every word.
# Chapter 3 - Case System & Alignment
## What Counts As An "Event"
Tsaqun is, as a language, very focused on **events** and the **resulting states** which come from said events. An event is a situation involving one or more participants, typically resulting in a change or state that is central to Tsaqun verb meaning.
## How Participants Are Encoded Structurally
Tsaqun distinguishes five core participant roles in verb argument structure.

- **S** - sole argument of an intransitive verb
- **A** - agent of a transitive or ditransitive verb
- **P** - patient of a transitive verb
- **T** - theme of a ditransitive verb
- **R** - recipient of a ditransitive verb
### Case Marking & Alignment

| Participant | Case           |
| ----------- | -------------- |
| Sole        | Absolutive     |
| Agent       | Ergative       |
| Patient     | Absolutive     |
| Theme       | Absolutive     |
| Recipient   | Dative/Oblique |
**Absolutive**
The absolutive marks the primary participant that the verb directly describes.

**Ergative**
The ergative marks the agent (A) of transitive and ditransitive verbs, indicating the participant that initiates or controls the action.

The presence of an ergative argument signals a causative/agentive reading.

**Dative/Oblique**
The dative/oblique case marks non-core or secondary participants such as recipients (R), goals, or other peripheral roles.
### Distribution Across Clause Types
The absolutive argument is the structural and semantic center of the clause. This is especially evident in the resultative aspect *(aq)* which describes the resulting state of the absolutive participant.

The ergative argument is often omitted freely in everyday discourse, especially when that information is evident from shared context.

**Intransitive clauses** have absolutive arguments.
**Transitive clauses** have absolutive and ergative arguments.
**Ditransitive clauses** have absolutive, ergative, and dative/oblique arguments.

Examples, with letter labels after respective participants:
- Ĝad(S) ghing - the man(S) is sleeping
- Ĝadu(A) qarq(P) nid-at - the man(A) saw the stone(P)
- Ĝadu(A) tsalkhi(R) dogh(T) ĝun-at - The man(A) gave an arctic hare(T) to the wolf(R)
# Chapter 4 - Verb System (Structure & Morphology)
## What Is A Verb
In Tsaqun a verb encodes an action or event, with special emphasis placed on the manner or mode of said action (often encoded lexically) and the aspect (encoded with post-verb particles). In particular, verbs imply a resulting state, and this state is central to Tsaqun discourse and cognitive framework.
## Root Structure
The basic structure is \[verb]-\[aspect/valency]-\[evidential].
## Semantic Types
Tsaqun verbs are typically far more precise that English verbs, encoding information such as path, terrain, force profile, structural outcome, and body state.
## Valency
Verbs differ in **valency**, defined as the number of core participants they require.

Verbs can be transitive, intransitive, or ditransitive. **Intransitive verbs** have a sole core participant. **Transitive verbs** have two core participants - an agent and a patient. **Ditransitive verbs** are verbs that have 3 participants - an agent, a theme, and a recipient.

The below table outlines valency across the verb types:

| Verb Type    | Participant |
| ------------ | ----------- |
| Intransitive | 1 (S)       |
| Transitive   | 2 (A, P)    |
| Ditransitive | 3 (A, T, R) |
## Aspect System
Verbs do not conjugate for tense, but rather have post-positioned aspect particles that specify completedness, continuity, and resulting state.

**Default Aspect**
The default aspect is expressed by not adding any particle. This aspect is used for general actions or anywhere the other aspects would not apply.

**Perfective Aspect**
The perfective aspect is marked with the particle *at*. The perfective aspect is used to express an action or event that has run its full course and is now **completed**.

**Imperfective Aspect**
This aspect is marked with the particle *as*, and is essentially the opposite of the above. The imperfective aspect is used for actions and events that are **currently ongoing**.

**Resultative Aspect**
The resultative aspect is annotated with the particle *aq*. It marks a verb as describing a present **state** resulting from a completed action; the predicate remains verbal and does not require a copula.
## Valency Encoding & Particle Morphology
Verbs encode valency in the post-positioned aspect particle. There are two distinct forms for each aspect: single argument, or multiple arguments.

|         | Perfective | Imperfective | Resultative |
| ------- | ---------- | ------------ | ----------- |
| 1 arg   | -at        | -as          | -aq         |
| 2+ args | -ut        | -us          | -uq         |
What this does is keep anti-passive and agent-suppressed sentences from devolving into ambiguity.

Examples:
- (full version) *Ĝadu baq nid-ut* - The man sees the sheep
- (anti-passive) *Ĝad baqi nid-at* - The man sees a sheep
- (reduced anti-passive) *Ĝad nid-at* - The man sees (something)
vs.
- (agent suppressed) *Baq nid-ut* - (Unspecified) sees the sheep

In the final, agent-suppressed version, the valency encoding in *-ut* prevents the listener from mistakenly inferring that the sheep is doing the seeing, as *-ut* implies a hidden agent performing the action on the absolutive argument.

Since the default verb aspect has no associated particle, valency is not encoded in said aspect. Because of this, context or added emphasis (including suppressed agents, etc.) must disambiguate where morphology does not.

## Vowel Final Verbs
Vowel-final verbs use an *-h-* to connect the end of the verb root with the aspect particle in fluid speech, like this:

- *Dirnĝu na danu-h-us* - The owl is watching me
## Mood/Modality
**Imperative**
The imperative is expressed with the particle *wa* (intense) and the particle *la* (soft). The two are mutually exclusive and come at the very end of the sentence.

Note that in imperative phrases that switch up the default word order, placing the patient after the verb rather than before. The full order is therefore \[verb] \[patient] \[imperative particle]. The agent is omitted in imperatives.

Examples:
- *Qhlek te wa!* - Strike it/him/her!
- *Ghing la* - Go to sleep (more gentle command)

Greater intensity can be conveyed by placing the patient back before the verb:
- *Te qhlek wa!* - Strike it!

**Ability/Possibility**
Ability/Possibility is expressed with the auxiliary verb *pogh*. *Pogh* covers two areas of modality. When no agent is present or apparent, it generally describes **possibility** (it might snow tomorrow). When an agent is in play, it describes **capability**.

Examples:
- *Jo pogh-at ghing* - I couldn't sleep
- *Pogh dughad* - We can move through the deep snow
- *Pogh nalĝ* - Maybe it is snowing

**Realis/Irrealis**
Realis, that is, what actually happens/happened, is the default in Tsaqun. Irrealis is a modality that is marked with the clause-initial particle *le*. Irrealis plays a central role in expressing tense-like meaning without a dedicated tense system; this is done by combining the irrealis particle with the various verb aspects.

Examples:
- *Le ghing* - I will sleep
- *Le ghing-at* - I would have slept
- *Le ghing-as* - I would be sleeping

It can combine with *pogh*:
- *Pogh le nalĝ* - Maybe it will snow

**Deontic (Obligation/Permission)**
There is the clause-level verb *qun* to express obligation, when something "must" be done. On the other hand, the verb *kej* expresses permission, i.e. "may". These verbs generally go at the very beginning of the entire clause, and do not function like auxiliaries.

Examples:
- *Kej ja sib* - You may drink
- *Qun nadlakh* - We have to travel
## Evidentiality
Tsaqun encodes evidentiality in its verb conjugations in the form of cliticized particles that come after the aspect/valency particle. Evidentiality specifies the source of the imparted information. Tsaqun has the following three evidentiality categories.

**Direct**
Direct evidentiality is the default and therefore is zero-marked (there is no particle). It applies to information obtained from direct personal experience (I saw, I heard, I witnessed, etc.).

**Inferred**
Inferred evidentiality is marked with the particle *-jus* and applies to information deduced, inferred, or assumed. This carries a moderately lower degree of certainty commitment than the direct default.

**Reported**
Reported evidentiality is marked with the particle *-ja* and applies to information obtained from a third party. It conveys the least degree of certainty/commitment when it comes to the accuracy/credibility of said information.

Examples:
- *Ĝad nash-at* - The man died of natural causes (I saw it)
- *Ĝad nash-at-jus* - The man died (I assumed/inferred it)
- *Ĝad nash-at-ja* - The man died (they told me so)

Evidentiality particles are non-obligatory, but are preferred when applicable, especially in more formal interactions and contexts.
## Voice Operations
### Anti-Passive
Antipassive reduces a transitive verb to an intransitive one by removing or demoting the patient (P). A transitive verb normally has this:
Agent (ergative) + Patient (absolutive) + Verb (transitive)

In antipassive voice:
Agent (absolutive) + P (oblique) + Verb (intransitive)

Semantically, antipassive is used where the object is unknown, irrelevant, or generic, so the focus shifts to the agent and the activity.

Example:
- Neutral: *Ĝadu tsalkh nid-ut* (The man saw the wolf)
- Antipassive: *Ĝad nid-at* (The man saw \[something])
- Anitpassive extended: *Ĝad tsalkhi nid-at* (The man saw a wolf)
### Agent Suppression (Impersonal Construction)
Tsaqun does not have a passive structure, but compensates with its agent suppression (impersonal construction). Normally a transitive verb has this:
Agent (ergative) + Patient (absolutive) + Verb (transitive)

In impersonal constructions, the verb is kept as a transitive verb and the agent is dropped.
Patient (absolutive) + Verb (transitive)

Semantically, agent suppression is used where the agent is unspecified, irrelevant, or generic, so the focus shifts to the patient and the activity.

Example:
- Neutral: *Ĝadu tsalkh nid-ut* (The man saw the wolf)
- Suppressed Agent: *Tsalkh nid-ut* (\[someone] saw the wolf)
### Causative
Causative operations are largely handled at the lexical level.
### Reflexive
The reflexive particle is *wa* and is used as the verb's patient when the agent and patient of a verbal clause are one and the same. The particle is placed right before the verb and functions like an absolutive noun patient. The reflexive particle is used for all grammatical persons does not encode number (single/plural).

Examples:
- (no reflexive particle) *Ĝadu qarq sluq-ut* - The man washed the stone
- (reflexive particle) *Ĝadu wa sluq-ut* - The man washed himself/bathed

Note that in the case of body parts, reflexive nature is not marked explicitly, but rather contextually understood. If contrast or emphasis are important, the possessive marker can be used.

- (no explicit marking) *Nu dos sluq-ut* - I washed my hands
- (explicitly marked) *Nu na hlu dos sluq-ut* - I washed *my* hands
- (non-default possessor) *Nu ĝad hlu dos sluq-ut* - I washed the man's hands
## Catenative Verbs & Valency Disambiguation
The verbs kure (“to want”) and walu (“to like/love”) are catenative verbs and may be followed directly by another verb. The second verb expresses an event and does not function as a core argument.

As a result, catenative verbs are treated as intransitive in these constructions.

Since the following verb appears in the default aspect and lacks explicit valency marking, some ambiguity may arise. This is resolved through word order, context, or explicit argument marking.

Examples:
- (suppressed agent) *Kure seĝ qas?* - (Do you) want to eat fish?
- (word order change) *Seĝ kure qas?* - Does the fish want to eat?
- (explicit agent) *Kure seĝ qas ju?* - Do you want to eat fish?

When no noun phrase appears before the catenative verb, the agent is typically inferred from context. A noun phrase in the preverbal position is interpreted as the subject. Explicit ergative marking disambiguates when necessary.

The verb walu expresses attachment, commitment, or repeated engagement. When used with a following verb, it extends metaphorically to activities:

- (catenative) *Na walu nadlakh* - I like traveling
- (non-catenative) *Nu ja walu* - I love you

**Note:** for inanimate objects, the verb belgh (“to please”) is used instead of *walu* and takes a dative experiencer:

- *Hlaĝ jo belgh* - I don’t like snow

Additionally, the verb *meq* ("to come/arrive") can be used as a catenative verb in conjunction with stative/adjectival verbs. See the chapter on modification for a thorough explanation with examples. 

# Chapter 5 - Noun Morphology & Participant Encoding
## Case Marking
### Absolutive Case
The absolutive case is zero-marked.
### Ergative Case
The ergative case is marked with the ending "-u".
### Dative/Oblique Case
The dative/oblique case is marked with the ending "-i".
### Case Particles
Further cases are specified by using a distinct case particle **in conjunction with** the oblique case ending. Each of the case particles **follow** the noun with the oblique case ending.

**Locative**
The locative case particle is *lor*.

Example:
- *Ĝad tokhduni lor dorq-at* - The man enters the cave

**Allative**
The allative case particle is *hla*.

Example:
- *Tsalkh tokhduni hla dughad-at* - The wolf moves throw snow toward the den

**Ablative**
The ablative case particle is *lakh*.

Example:
- *Tsiruqhi lakh tang-at* - I fled from the wolverine

**Benefactive/Purpose**
The benefactive/purpose case particle is *kru*.

Example:
- *Nu ji kru dalkh le ĝeln-ut* - I will blow the fire for you
- *Tarq-at kru wa pahl* - He sat down to rest himself

**Instrumental/Accompaniment**
The instrumental case particle is *r*. The instrumental case is used to describe the means by which an action is accomplished, or with what tool/implement.

Example:
- *Ĝadu tsalkh qarqi r qhlek-ut* - The man struck the wolf with a rock
- *Ĝadu tsalkhi r nadlakh-as* - The man is traveling with the wolf
## Plurality
Tsaqun does not grammatically distinguish singular vs plural. All nouns (and pronouns) are number-neutral unless modified.

The word *tso* marks non-singular (more than one) nouns, **not** plurality as a grammatical category.

The number *da* (one) can be used with nouns to specify a single noun.

Examples:
- *Nu tohlaq nid* - I see ibex (number unspecified)
- *Nu tso tohlaq nid* - I see ibices (plural patient)
- *Nu da tohlaq nid* - I see an ibex (singular patient)

Keep in mind that *tso* (and in its own right *da*) is used only contrastively or emphatically, not every single time there are multiple of a noun. In most cases no quantifier is used and context disambiguates.
## Possession
Attributive possession is indicated with the linker particle *hlu*, with the possessor preceding the particle, which precedes the thing being possessed.

Example:
- *Ĝad hlu qal duraĝ-at* - The man's raven fell

Predicative possession is expressed with the dative case + *went* ("there is"/"exists").

Example:
- *Ni seĝ went* - I have (some) trout

Relying on shared context, omitting the dative noun is also an option for brevity's sake.

- *Seĝ went* - There is/(I have) some trout
## Animacy
Tsaqun distinguishes between animate and inanimate referents. Animate referents include humans and animals, and are more likely to be interpreted as agents in events. Inanimate referents include objects, terrain, and environmental features, and are more typically treated as patients or undergoers of actions. This distinction influences interpretation and verb compatibility, but does not result in extensive morphological marking.
## Pronouns
**Forms**
When it comes to Tsaqun pronouns there are 2 persons - 1st and 2nd. As with all other nouns, pronouns are *number-neutral* i.e. they are neither explicitly plural nor singular.

|            | Pronoun |
| ---------- | ------- |
| 1st person | na      |
| 2nd person | ja      |

When distinction of number is important in discourse, the word *tso* is used just as with regular nouns. *Tso na* refers to the speaker and others they are with, and *tso ja* refers to multiple people the speaker is addressing. Again, these explicit forms are not default in every day speech, but rather used emphatically when overt disambiguation is important.

Tsaqun does not employ dedicated third-person pronouns. Instead, demonstrative determiners are used to refer to third-person entities. See the subsection on determiners for further information.

**Case Behavior**
Pronouns encode grammatical case morphologically, that is, their form changes based the case they are in.

|            | Absolutive | Ergative | Dative/Oblique |
| ---------- | ---------- | -------- | -------------- |
| 1st person | na         | nu       | ni             |
| 2nd person | ja         | ju       | ji             |

**Usage**
Pronouns are used in sentences just like regular nouns. Pronouns are frequently omitted when their referent is recoverable from context. This is especially common for agents in transitive clauses, where verb morphology already indicates the presence of an agent.

Examples:
- *Ju tsalkh qhlek-ut?* - You struck the wolf?
- (suppressed agent) *Tsalkh qhlek-ut?* - (You) struck the wolf? (agent apparent from context, questioning nature of sentence)
## Determiners
Tsaqun employs determiners *ta* and *te* in referential or contrastive contexts. *Ta* is used with **animate** nouns and *te* is used with **inanimate** nouns. Determiners always precede the noun they modify.

Examples:
- *Ta ĝent ghing-as* - That/the (contextually identifiable) lynx is sleeping
- *Te qarq tsraq-at* - That/the (contextually identifiable) rock snapped (under load)

When used together with nouns, the noun is declined but the determiner is not.

When used without a noun, determiners function as third-person referential elements and decline as a noun/pronoun. When declined as a pronoun, *ta/te* only maintains the animate-inanimate distinction in the absolutive case.

| Case           | Form  |
| -------------- | ----- |
| Absolutive     | ta/te |
| Ergative       | tu    |
| Dative/Oblique | ti    |

Examples:
- *Tu te tsraq-ut* - He/she/they snapped it
- *(Nu) ti te qarq ĝun-ut* - (I) gave him that rock
## Numbers & Counting
When standing in for nouns, numbers decline based on case.  *Da* and *qa* declensions rely on vowel alternation, while all other numbers use suffixation like regular nouns.

| Numeral | Absolutive | Ergative | Dative/Oblique |
| ------- | ---------- | -------- | -------------- |
| 1       | da         | du       | di             |
| 2       | qa         | qu       | qi             |
| 3       | sar        | saru     | sari           |
| 4       | hlet       | hletu    | hleti          |
| 5       | bigh       | bighu    | bighi          |
| 6       | tek        | teku     | teki           |
| 7       | neq        | nequ     | neqi           |
| 8       | bas        | basu     | basi           |
| 9       | nogh       | noghu    | noghi          |

## Interrogatives
The interrogative particle is *unq*, meaning "what" or "which". Compositional forms combine *unq* with other words to ask different questions.


| tsaqun   | meaning            |
| -------- | ------------------ |
| unq      | what/which\*       |
| unq khup | what place (where) |
| unq las  | what manner (how)  |
| unq kru  | what for (why)     |
\**Unq* means "what" by itself and "which" before a noun, or when standing in for a noun.

Interrogatives shift default word order from \[noun phrase] \[preverbal slot] \[verb] to \[preverbal slot (interrogative)] \[verb] \[noun phrase], although stylistically the noun phrase can still precede the interrogative. The main thing is that the interrogative occupies the preverbal spot as the focused element **unless** something else is being emphasized.

Examples:
- *Unq qas?* - What are you eating?
- *Unq seĝ qas?* - Which fish are you eating?
- *Unq khup ghing ĝad?* - Where is the man sleeping?
- *Unq khup ĝad ghing?* Where is *the man* sleeping?
- *Unq las sluq seĝ?* - How are you washing the fish?
- *Unq kru tang-at?* - Why did you run?
## Yes/No
Yes is *jaq*. No is *jo*.
# Chapter 9 - Clause-Level Particles
## Negation
The basic negative particle is *jo*. It is used both as a simple response to yes/no questions, and as a negative particle in verb phrases. The negative particle takes the place right before the verb or noun being negated.

Examples:
- *Ĝeldig jo nid* - I don't see the caribou
- *Jo ĝeldig nid* - It's not the caribou that I see

Tsaqun does not have dedicated negative pronouns. Instead, speakers use the negative particle jo with certain general nouns—most commonly ĝad (“person”) and qan (“thing”)—to express meanings equivalent to “nobody” and “nothing.”

In this usage, *jo* and the noun form a tight phonological unit with primary stress on jo. This construction functions as a negative indefinite and differs from standard clausal negation.

In this grammar, these forms are written with a hyphen (jo-ĝad, jo-qan) to reflect their prosodic unity, though they are not morphologically bound.

Examples:
- *Ĝad jo nid* - I don't see the man
- *Jo ĝad nid* - It's the man I don't see
- *Jo-ĝad nid* - I see no man
- *Jo-qan nid* - I see no object

The general rule is that negation takes scope over the element it immediately precedes. The following examples demonstrate how *jo* interacts with words like *pogh*, *kej*, and *qun*.

Examples:
- *Jo pogh nadlakh* - We cannot travel
- *Jo pogh jo nadlakh* - We can't not travel
- *Pogh le jo nalĝ* - Maybe it won't snow
- *Jo qun ghing* - You don't have to sleep
- *Qun jo ghing* - You must not sleep
- *Jo kej ghing* - You are not allowed to sleep
- *Kej jo ghing* - You are allowed to not sleep

# Chapter 10 - Clause Linking & Subordination
## Coordination
**Cataphoric Placeholders & Subordination**
Tsaqun uses a **cataphoric placeholder** *do* as the patient of a verb before a transitive clause, and then uses *tse* after the verb to introduce the subordinated clause.

\[*do*] \[verb] \[*tse*] \[subordinated clause]

Examples:
- *Do nado tse waqhuqu seĝ qas* - I know that bears eat trout
- *Do nid tse tsalkhu ĝad qas-us* - I see that the wolf is eating the man

Either just *tse* can be omitted, or both *tse* and *do*, depending on the context (formal vs. informal) and ambiguity risk.

Examples:
- (slightly reduced) *Do nado ja ta ĝadi r tsaqun-at* - I know you communicated with that man
- (fully reduced) *Nid ja nash-as* - I see you're dying

## Participant Tracking
Tsaqun uses participant tracking particles to keep track of which previously mentioned participant continues as the primary actor of the next clause. The particle *to* refers to the agent of the original clause, and the particle *ho* refers to the patient or theme of the original clause. If there is a recipient in the original clause, the particle *ri* can be used.

Examples:
- *Ĝadu tsalkh nid-ut a to ghing-at* - The man saw the wolf and (the man) slept
- *Ĝadu tsalkh nid-ut a ho ghing-at* - The man saw the wolf and (the wolf) slept
- *Ĝadu tsalkhi seĝ ĝun-ut a ri ta qas-at* - The man gave the wolf a trout and the wolf ate it REVISE(?)

The particles do not decline for other cases, even when used in such contexts:
- *Ĝadu tsalkh nid-ut a ho ta nid-ut* - The man saw the wolf and (the wolf) saw him
