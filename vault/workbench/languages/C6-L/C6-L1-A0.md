# Chapter 1 - Typology & Phonology
## Overview
Jety is a polysynthetic language that incorporates nouns into conjugated verb forms - entire sentences are represented by a single word. It has split intransitive (actor vs undergoer distinction), no tense but heavy aspect, animacy sensitive nominal number, and verb-final default word order (SOV). It is an oral-only language as writing was not yet invented in A0.

**Note on written language:** Jety in A0 did not yet have a written form, therefore, in this grammar, we will be using roman alphabet approximations to represent the spoken language.

### Allophony
Certain phonemes are pronounced differently based on context. As a general rule, consonants are pronounced in two distinct ways, depending on whether they are followed by a front vowel or a back vowel. In the case of word- or phrase-final consonants (no vowel after), the pronunciation is determined by the preceding vowel.

**Back Vowel (a, o, u) (default)**
When a consonant is followed by a back vowel (or no vowel) it defaults to the back articulation.
- k -> voiceless uvular plosive
- g -> voiced uvular plosive
- s -> alveolar sibilant fricative
- h -> voiceless glottal fricative
- n -> voiced alveolar nasal

**Front Vowel (e, i, y)**
When a consonant is followed by a front vowel, it is pronounced with front articulation.
- k -> voiceless velar plosive
- g -> voiced velar plosive
- s -> voiceless postalveolar fricative
- h -> voiceless palatal fricative
- n -> voiced palatal nasal

Note that the above consonants follow the stated rules even when another consonant, such as *j* or *l* or *r*, lies in between the original consonant and the vowel in question.
### Phonotactics
The most common syllable shapes are as follows:
- CVC
- CV
- CCV, CCVC, or CVCC (least common, and ONLY when one of the two juxtaposed consonants is *r*, *l*, or *j*)
### Stress & Vowel Length
In general the main stress is placed on the initial syllable of a word. However, there are frequent exceptions dictated by predictable patterns.

**Verbal Units**
In verbal units the stress is placed on the first syllable of the verb root itself; i.e., the initial syllable (most commonly a verbal personal prefix) does not receive the main stress, but rather the verb root. This also applies in the case of incorporated nouns - the verb root, not the noun root, receives the stress.

**Long Vowels**
Long vowels override all other considerations and draw stress to themselves. These are marked with a ":" after the vowel being lengthened. Some morphological units contain multiple long vowels, in which case stress is placed on each long vowel, giving the unit a very particular rhythm (viz. *i:tonu:dane* - "in this valley").
# Chapter 2 - Core Morphological Architecture
## Verbal Unit Structure
Verbal units are the most load-bearing morphological units in Jety. They encode as much as an entire sentence in a single word. This includes participants and participant roles (prefix), any incorporated nouns, the verb root itself, and aspect suffixes.

\[(negative prefix)]\[verbal prefix]\[(incorporated noun)]\[verb root]\[aspect]

**Negative Prefix**
The negative prefix is *hi:-*.

## Nominal Unit Structure
Sometimes additional information must be conveyed separately from the main verbal unit. In this case nominal constructions are used, which can include demonstrative prefixes, the noun root itself (always), dual/plural endings, and case endings.

\[(demonstrative)]\[noun root]\[(dual/plural)]\[(case)]
# Chapter 3 - Nominal Morphology
## Nominal Number
Jety often distinguishes between 3 grammatical numbers - single, dual, and plural.

Jety speakers differentiate between animate and inanimate subjects. Humanoids and animals are considered animate. Everything else is inanimate. Nominal number is the main place where this distinction comes into play.

**Animate Nouns**
Animate nouns have dedicated dual *(-rak)* and plural *(-ri:ko)* endings. Take *hus* (fish) and *lahy* (deer) for example:
- single: *hus, lahy*
- dual: *husrak, lahyrak*
- plural: *husri:ko, lahyri:ko*

**Inanimate Nouns**
Inanimate nouns have only an eroded plural form *-e*. For *kep* (stone) and *nel* (tree):
- single: *kep, nel*
- plural: *kepe, nele*
## Demonstratives
Jety has a single demonstrative prefix *i:to-*. It attaches to the front of any noun being referenced:
- *i:toketeg* - this/that branch
- *i:todo:ken* - this/that den
## Locative & Spatial Suffixes
Jety has a very light case system with only 3 marked case endings. These attach to the ends of nouns as suffixes.

**Locative**
The locative case ending is *-ne* and generally means "in/on/at" based on context.

**Allative**
The allative case ending is *-da* and means "to/toward/in the direction of".

**Ablative**
The ablative case ending is *-ja* and means "from/away from".

# Chapter 4 - Verbal Prefixes
## Overview
There are 3 different categories of verbal prefixes in Jety. The first is actor prefixes, which are used for intransitive (single argument) verbs that embody **action taken**. The second is undergoer prefixes, which are used for intransitive verbs that embody **processes undergone**. The distinction between these types of verbs will be explored further in the subsection titled **Split Intransitivity.**

The third and final category is relational prefixes. These are the most complex and numerous of all. They encode relationships between multiple arguments in transitive verbs.

**Number**
As noted in the chapter on nouns, Jety distinguishes between single, dual, and plural numbers.

**Inclusive vs. Exclusive Person**
Jety distinguishes between an inclusive and exclusive dual and plural 1st person. Inclusive includes the speaker's addressee (& co) while exclusive excludes them.

## Split Intransitivity
Since there are two categories of verbal prefix that both apply to intransitive (single argument) verbs, it is important to understand which types of verbs take an actor prefix, and which ones take an undergoer prefix.

**Actor Prefix**
Used for verbs like hunt, walk, jump, eat, see. Actor is volitional, controlled, agentive.

**Undergoer Prefix**
Used for verbs like sleep, fall, die, live, breathe, etc. Undergoer is a state, involuntary process, affectedness.
# Chapter 5 - Verb Root Semantics

## Root Classes
Jety verbs falls into three classes. There are **neutral verbs** (the most common), **a-verbs**, and **u-verbs**. U-verbs are an irregular lexical subclass of alternating verbs.

Neutral verb roots have stable (unchanged) morphology across all contexts.

The other two verb types are explained in the sections below.
## Vowel Alternation (Specificity)
A-verbs and U-verbs both have alternating vowels based on the specificity or individuatedness of the thing being acted upon (viz deer-hunting vs hunting a/the deer). The default vowel for both classes is -e-.
### A-Verbs
A-verbs are more numerous than and form the foundation for u-verbs. Essentially a-verbs have a default form using -e- as their main vowel in contexts where the patient of the verb is nonspecific or unindividuated. When the patient is individuated the vowel of the verb changes from -e- to -a- as follows.

- *Nelahyderlu* - I am deer-hunting
- *Silahydarlu* - I am hunting a deer

Note that this can harmonize with noun incorporation (covered in chapter 6). When the a-form is used with an incorporated noun, it individuates the noun even while it is incorporated, making a subtle but important shift in meaning.

- *Nelahyderlu* - I am deer-hunting
- *Silahydarlu* - I am hunting a deer
- *Lahy sidarlu* - I am hunting the deer
### U-Verbs
U-verbs are a special subclass of alternating verbs where the default -e- vowel alternates either to -a- or -u- based on intended semantic meaning. The e-form is once again the generic, nonspecific form, with the a-form being used by default when the target is a concrete individual thing.

The u-form is employed to convey a semantically related but distinct meaning. The additional meaning is somewhat arbitrary and can't necessarily be predicted based on any pattern, but this group of verbs is so finite that they will be listed here in full with explanations and examples.

**Der/dar/dur**
*Der* - to hunt
*Dar* - to hunt something
*Dur* - to stalk something

Examples:
- *Nehulyderlu* - I am hunting bird/I am bird-hunting
- *Huly sidarlu* - I am hunting the bird
- *Lahy sidurlu* - I am stalking the deer

**Hed/had/hud**
*Hed* - to move by carrying or dragging
*Had* - to carry something
*Hud* - to drag something

Examples:
- *Surekephedlu* - They are moving rocks/they are rock-moving
- *Kepri:ko sasahaduk* - They carried the rocks
- *Kepri:ko sasahuduk* - They dragged the rocks

**Hek/hak/huk**
*Hek* - to call out
*Hak* - to call someone
*Huk* - to call someone back

Examples:
- *Kurehekuk* - You two called out
- *Kanahakuk* - You called us
- *Sakuhukuk* - They called you two back

**Hel/hal/hul**
*Hel* - to hear
*Hal* - to hear something/someone
*Hul* - to listen to something/someone

Examples:
- *Nehellu* - I hear
- *Nesu:lohallu* - I hear a river
- *Nesu:lohullu* - I am listening to the river

**Jes/jas/jus**
*Jes* - to guard
*Jas* - to guard something/someone
*Jus* - to watch over/care for something/someone

Examples:
- *Kejeslu* - You are guarding
- *Do:ken kesijasletu* - You are guarding the den
- *Nelanrak kesijusuk* - You cared for the (two) children

**Rek/rak/ruk**
*Rek* - to track
*Rak* - to track/follow something
*Ruk* - to search for signs of something

Examples:
- *Narasortarekhe* - We are about to track wolves
- *Sigurtaraklu* - I am tracking a bear
- *Gurta sasarukuk* - They searched for signs of the bear

**Ren/ran/run**
*Ren* - to see
*Ran* - to see/spot/make out something
*Run* - to watch something

Examples:
- *Surenlu* - He sees
- *Sanaranlu* - He sees us
- *Sanarunlu* - He is watching us

**Tek/tak/tuk**
*Tek* - to strike/make impact
*Tak* - to strike (swinging) something
*Tuk* - to strike (thrusting/piercing) something

Examples:
- *Neteklu* - I strike
- *Sorta sikepetekletu* - I strike the wolf with a rock repeatedly
- *Gurta sisuraketekjat* - I thrust the bear with a spear
## Root Alternation Summary

| e-form                    | a-form                 | u-form                                      |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| default, non-individuated | individuated, specific | individuated + related but distinct meaning |

# Chapter 6 - Noun Incorporation

## Formal Structure
The basic structure of a verb with an incorporated noun is as follows:

\[prefix]\[noun]\[verb]\[aspect]
## Semantic Function
Noun incorporation backgrounds the noun and foregrounds the event.

With incorporation, the phrase becomes activity-centered, while without incorporation it remains participant-centered.

Examples:
- *Gurta siraklu* - I'm tracking the bear
- *Sigurtareklu* - I am bear-tracking
- *Sigurtaraklu* - I'm tracking a bear
## Redundant Explicit Noun Phrase (Emphasis)
Even when a noun is being incorporated, it can be redundantly repeated outside of the verbal unit for emphasis, like so:

- *Sisortaranlu sorta* - I see a *wolf*
## Instrument Incorporation
Noun incorporation can also be used to incorporate an instrument used to accomplish a task. Any patient is kept external in this case.

Example:
- *Kety sisurakekatjat* - I killed the predator with a spear
## Locative Incorporation
Intransitive verbs in particular can use noun incorporation to specify locative information.

- *Nudo:kenesanhe* - We are about to "den-sleep"
- *Neneleno:latlu* - I am taking shelter (under) a tree
# Chapter 7 - Derivational Morphology
- Attributive: -y (instrumental or agentive)
- Patient/result (thing that was X'd): -or
- Action/event noun (the act of X): zero marked
- Locative (place of X): -las
- State/property (one that is X-ing): -ta
## Overview
Verbs in Jety can receive various derivational endings that transform them into nouns. The derivational endings each attach directly to the end of a verb root.

There is also a verb stem extension that will be covered at the end of the chapter.

## Attributive Ending
The attributive ending *-y* creates a noun identified by its attribute described by the verb root. Many such derived nouns are distinct lexicalized terms, but the ending can be used very productively to describe less common objects on the fly.

- *Ket -> kety* - predator, killer
- *Lah -> lahy* - deer, literally grazer
- *Ropat -> ropaty* - roamer
- *Sakan -> sakany* - cutting implement, literally cutter
## Patientive/Resultative Ending
The patientive/resultative ending is *-or* and indicates something that was X'd, where X = the verb root.

- *Ket -> ketor* - a kill (as in the thing killed)
- *Ren -> renor* - a sight, a view
- *Der -> deror* - prey ("hunted")
## Action/Event Nouns
Action/event nouns derived from verbs are zero-marked, i.e. they are morphological identical to the verb root they derive from .

- *Ket -> ket* - killing (the activity)
- *Hus -> hus* - swimming (note this also overlaps with the noun *hus* which means fish)
- *Ker -> ker* - the chase/pursuit
## Locative Ending
The locative ending *-las* marks "place of X" where X = the verb root. It can be used for expressions like the following.

- *Ket -> ketlas* - the killing ground
- *San - sanlas* - the sleeping spot
- *Kis -> kislas* - the deathplace
## Stative/Property Ending
The stative/property ending *-ta* creates words that mean "one who X's" where X = the verb root. These can be distinct lexical items or simply improvised derivational descriptors.

- *Sor -> sorta* - wolf (literally one who smells)
- *Sakan -> sakanta* - one who cuts up (or butcher)
- *Tal -> talta* - one who climbs (or climber)
## Verb Stem Extension (Volitional Intensifier)
*-ru-* can be inserted into any verb root to add intensity and/or deliberation to its meaning. *-ru-* means deep, committed enactment.

- *Ser -> serur* - move across -> cross A to B (specific destination)
- *Tas -> tarus* - to snap in half -> to snap in half more intensely
- *Ket -> kerut* - to kill -> to finish decisively, to execute

Note that the *-ru-* intensifier can also be combined with derivational endings such as *-ta* to create more specific nouns.

- *Kerutta* - executioner
# Chapter 8 - Aspect System
## Overview
Jety has no verb tense, but features a very thorough aspect system. Aspect is marked with a suffix that attaches directly to the end of the verb root. Aspect is non-optional.

In the following sections each of the 7 aspects will be described, with accompanying examples.
## Perfective
Marked with *-uk*. Views an event as a bounded whole. Not necessarily "successful". Used for discrete actions, narrating sequential events, and impactful bounded events.

- *Lahy sitakuk* - I struck the deer
- *Nu:dane suseruk* - He crossed the valley
- *Keteg sytasuk* - The branch snapped

## Imperfective
Marked with *-lu*. Views an event internally, as ongoing or unbounded. Can cover current process or broader ongoing activity. Not habitual. Used when action in progress, ongoing state, or active process.

- *Lahy siraklu* - I am tracking the deer
- *Sysanlu* - He sleeps (currently)
- *Su:dane sureserlu* - They move through the valley (ongoing process)

## Habitual
Marked with *-letu*. Regular repeated behavior or characteristic pattern. Not current-event focused. Used for repeated survival behaviors, routines, and seasonal patterns.

- *I:tonu:dane nasilahyderletu* - We hunt deer in this valley
- *I:todo:kenne sarysanletu* - They sleep in this den
- *Syltakety ji:na suropatletu* - The syltakety circles here

## Resultative
Marked with *-syko*. Current state caused by prior event; focus on resulting condition. Not the event itself. Used when describing aftermath, emphasizing achieved state.

- *Lahy sukissyko* - The deer is dead
- *Tarja susakansyko* - The meat is cut

## Iterative
Marked with *-lata*. Repeated event occurrences. Not general habit. Used for repeated strikes, repeated calls, repeated scanning, etc.

- *Sutaklata* - He struck repeatedly
- *Suheklata* - She called again and again
- *Syltakety suropatlata* - The pterosaur circled repeatedly

## Prospective
Marked with *-he*. Event is imminent or intended. Near future without tense. Used when preparing to attack, preparing movement, preparing to sleep.

- *Nenythe* - I am about to jump
- *Sutakhe* - He is about to strike
- *Sarysanhe* - They are about to sleep

## Completive
Marked with *-jat*. Event reached intended endpoint. Goal achieved. Used for successful kills, finished eating, finished crossing, fully repaired.

- *Negeljat* - I finished eating
- *Sesekatjat* - He killed it fully
- *Nereserjat* - We completed the crossing
# Chapter 9 - Modifiers
Jety does not feature a distinct adjective or adverb class. Instead, it uses verbs - especially stative verbs - to express qualities of nouns and actions.

These stative verbs are ordinary verbal units and take undergoer prefixes. The placement of a stative verb depends on its function in the sentence.

## Property Predication
When used after nouns in simple clauses, stative verbs function as predicative property expressions.

- *Lahy syjolanlu* - The deer is large
## Nominal modification
When a stative verb precedes a noun, it modifies that noun. It functions similarly to an attributive adjective, but structurally it is a reduced relative-clause-like modifier.

- *Syjolanlu lahy sunipegellu* - The big deer is berry-eating

Focused postverbal constituents retain their modifier relationships (see chapter 10 on focus position).
## Resultative Modification
Using the resultative aspect, modifier-like verbs can be used to describe an end state rather than the inherent quality or process of the verb's meaning.

Note that regular event verbs are preferred over stative verbs for this kind of modification, since the state-like quality comes from the resultative aspect, not from the verb itself.

- *Nel sytorsyko* - The tree is fallen
- *Sytorsyko nel senesarjak* - The fallen tree is blocking me
## Event Modification
The same stative verbs that modify nouns can generally be used to modify events as well. When a stative verb precedes another verb, it modifies the manner or quality of that event.

- *Nysitlu netallu* - I climb quickly (*sit* - be fast)
- *Lahy sysuhellu sugellu* - The deer is eating quietly
## The Key Rule
Across all the above use cases, the principle remains the same - **except in predication, a stative verb modifies the immediately following constituent.**

# Chapter 10 - Clause Structure & Syntax
## Basic Clause Order
The unmarked clause order in Jety is Subject Object Verb (SOV), with the verb in the final position. Overt noun phrases precede the verbal unit unless pragmatically marked for focus.

**Intransitive clauses** have only S and V, therefore they are S-V patterned by default. In the case of no external subject (prefix marks subject) it is simply V.

- *Nelan sysanlu* - The child is sleeping (undergoer intranstiive)
- *Jot sutallu* - The man is climbing (actor intransitive)
- *Nykislu* - I am dying (undergoer intransitive, no external subject)

**Transitives** follow S-O-V order or, in the case of an incorporated noun, S-V with V containing the object, or simply V if V contains the object and the subject is marked in the prefix.

- *Jot to:rag sesetaluk* - The man climbed the mountain
- *Jot seseto:ragetaluk* - The man mountain-climbed
- *Seseto:ragetaluk* - He/She mountain-climbed

**Ditransitives** (3 participants) still follow the same core order and structure. They are discussed in the section of the same name at the end of this chapter.
## Focus Position
The focus position in Jety sentences is the position right after the verb. This is used to emphasize whatever word is placed in the focus position, contrasting sharply with the default verb-final clause structure.

- *Nelan sysanlu* -> *Sysanlu nelan* - The *child* is sleeping
- *Nelahyderlu* -> *Nelahyderlu no:ta* - *I* am deer-hunting
## Overt Noun Phrase Omission
A common practice in Jety speech is the omission of overt noun phrases, especially when noun incorporation is used and/or context and the verbal prefix disambiguate the participants and their roles. This is especially true for 1st and 2nd person participants, who are only named with explicit pronouns when providing emphasis or contrast (see subsection on pronouns below).
## Ditransitives
Ditransitive verbs are ones like *nag* (give) that have an **agent (A)**, a **theme (T)**, and a **recipient (R).** In ditransitives, the verbal prefix indexes agent and recipient. The theme remains external. When the recipient is overtly expressed, it is commonly marked with the allative case.

When all three nouns are external, explicit participants, word order and semantics heavily influence default interpretation of roles, with the default order being A-R-T-V (V being the verb).

- *Tarja kinaguk* - I gave you meat
- *Jot nelanda tarja sesenaguk* - The man gave the child the meat
### Benefactive Particle
In benefactive ditransitive phrases a particle is used to explicitly mark an external noun beneficiary and disambiguate this from a recipient.

- *Ke:ta do lahy siketuk* - I killed the deer for you
## Pronouns
Pronouns are very lightweight in Jety and are only used for emphasis, as verbs already encode participants without external referents.

| person      | pronoun |
| ----------- | ------- |
| 1sg/1du/1pl | no:ta   |
| 2sg/2du/2pl | ke:ta   |
| 3sg         | so:ta   |
| 3du/3pl     | so:rata |
## Interrogative Constructions
### Interrogative Pronouns
The following interrogative pronouns are used for asking content questions in Jety.

| meaning | pronoun  |
| ------- | -------- |
| who     | do:na    |
| what    | hu:na    |
| where   | hu:nalas |
| when    | pa:ni    |
| why     | hu:naben |
| how     | ho:no    |
### Polar Questions
Polar questions are marked with the particle *o:ni* at the very beginning of the question phrase.

- *O:ni nulahyderhe?* - Are we about to go deer-hunting?

### Negative/Affirmative Particles
The affirmative particle is *a:ja* and the negative particle is *hi:nu*.
### Content Questions
Content questions such as "Who approaches?" or "What are you eating?" follow the same word order as declarative phrases, with the key difference being the use of an interrogative pronoun at the very beginning of the phrase.
