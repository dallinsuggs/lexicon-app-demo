# C5-L1-A0
[[culture-C5]]
## Overview
# Chapter - Typology
C5-L1-A0 is an analytical language with remnants of a historical inflection system. While only three productive case endings remain (nominative, accusative, and genitive), prepositions and fixed word order compensate for the less complex overt morphology.

An inherited lexical stress system combines with sound changes over time to produce a phonological stress pattern in words that gives C5-L1-A0 its unique rhythm and sound.
# Chapter - Phonology & Stress
**Note on written language:** C5-L1 in A0 did not yet have a written form, therefore, in this grammar, we will be using roman alphabet approximations to represent the spoken language.

C5-L1-A0's consonants are similar to those of its parent language. Vowels, however, underwent a significant shift over the years.
## Consonants
Consonants are as follows.

- b
- d
- f
- g - hard "g" sound like "go"
- h
- k
- m
- n
- p
- r
- s
- t
- v
## Vowels
C5-L1-A0 inherited roots from a language where word stress followed specific rules. Over time the vowels of the words shifted unequally - stressed vowels changed one way, and unstressed vowels centralized, becoming less distinct.

**Stressed Vowels**
- o - as in American English "boat", but no diphthong
- a - as in American English "cot"
- é - as in French "été"
- i - as in French "pipe"
- ai - as in American English "my"

**Unstressed Vowels**
- e - as in French "même" or "nette"
- ö - as in French "deux" or "blue" - lips relaxed, not tight
- u - as in American English "sun", "but", or "plus"
## Stress
As mentioned above, lexical stress (per-word stress) always falls on the stressed vowel, of which there will typically be no more than one outside of compound words. Some smaller words and particles may have no stressed vowel, and as such, in usage are deemphasized (prepositions like *kö* and *uf* for example).

This lexical stress **does not shift** when case endings or verb conjugations are attached. However, some declined root forms are themselves lexicalized and the stress has shifted, in particular with personal pronouns. It is always obvious when this is the case sense each of the vowels before the final will be vowels from the unstressed category.
# Chapter - Nouns
Nouns name a person, place, or thing. Historically nouns had extensive case morphology tracking grammatical role in a sentence, but by Age 0 C5 nouns have lost all but a few case distinctions.
## Case Endings
The only remaining explicitly marked cases are nominative, accusative, and genitive. Nouns that end in a vowel are declined by inserting a *-d-* in between the end of the root and the case ending (except for the genitive inanimate case).
### Nominative Case
The nominative case is marked with *-ai* on all animate nouns, while inanimate nouns are not marked.

- *Berapai féder maiteni* - The chicken eats the seeds
- *Tok lékör tukait* - Stone is hard
### Accusative Case
The accusative case is marked with *-i* on both animate and inanimate nouns.

- *Mönai nödop kéli* - We catch fish
- *Urai merétenör tokeni* - He is gathering stones
### Genitive Case
The genitive case is marked with *-o* for animate, *-s* for inanimate nouns.

- *Nugaig dapeks danör gaiti kö faid* - The boy's shadow covered the object on the ground
- *Fairelai dapeko léknir bekét* - The boy's father was upset
- *Hölon föréns lékör bais* - The water of the river is clear
## Number
The plural is marked with the ending *-en* on all verbs. Case endings attach directly to the end of the plural marker as appropriate.

- *Fai litu daipeni* - I see the crows
# Chapter - Verbs
With verb endings aggressively eroded, personal pronouns become much more important in day-to-day speech. They are almost always required unless the context is especially disambiguating.
## Present Tense
The only two grammatical person/number combinations that maintain distinct endings are first and third person singular. Everything else is zero marked. While this does mean clarity comes from accompanying pronouns, it does not mean that pronouns are necessarily required. Depending on context and shared understanding, pronouns are still frequently omitted, especially in casual one-on-one interactions.

- *Fai tigu* - I go
- *Gai tig* - You go
- *Urai tigör* - He/she/it goes
- *Mönai tig* - We go
- *Dölönai tig* - You all go
- *Hunai tig* - They go

Note that the 3rd person singular ending is reduced to a simple *-r* when attaching to roots that end in a vowel, i.e. *féder*.

So if a son is speaking with his father and his father asks him to go take care of something, it would be very natural for the son to simply reply *Tigu* without the pronoun.
## Past Tense
The past tense conjugation is identical across all persons and numbers: *-nir*. Here pronouns are often much more enforced.

- *Fai tignir* - I went
- *Mönai tignir* - We went
## Imperative Verbs
Imperative verbs are simply the regular verb stem without any personal ending.

- *Tig!* - Go!
- *Bafer!* - Jump!

This covers both 2nd person singular and 2nd person plural address. To express a 1st person plural imperative ("let's..."), the following is used.

- *Mönai buras!* - Let's drink!
## Infinitives
The infinitive marker is *-(e)r*. Infinitive verbs are used in conjunction with auxiliaries to express ideas like the following. Verbs ending in a vowel receive simply an *-r*.

- *Fai lebétu baferer* - I want to jump
- *Fairelai naikör föraser* - Father is trying to sleep
# Chapter - Modifiers
In C5 adjectives always precede the noun they modify. While most adjective-noun agreement is gone from the language, adjectives *do* take the accusative ending *-i* when modifying nouns in the accusative case, and the plural marker is used on adjectives marking plural nouns.

- *Urai litnir duraigeni berapi* - He saw the big chicken
- *Duraigenen berapenai féde* - The big chickens eat

Adjectives can function by themselves as a noun when the actual modified noun is omitted. In these cases the adjective declines with nominal case endings.

- *Duraigenai féder* - The big one eats
- *Holenai sal* - The wet ones slipped
## Deriving Adjectives From Nouns
There are two productive derivational endings for creating adjectives from nouns.
### Attributive *-su*
The ending *-su* creates an adjective describing something as having the properties of the noun from which it is formed. English equivalent is often "-ish" or "-y".

- *gelér -> gelérsu* - girl -> girlish
- *tain -> tainsu* - milk -> milky
- *zölaren -> zölarsu* - hair(s) -> hairy
### Comparative *-ud*
The ending *-ud* creates an adjective meaning "X-like", where "X" is the noun root.

- *kél -> kélud* - fish -> fish-like
## Adjective Agreement With Nouns
Adjectives agree with the nouns they modify grammatical number, and to a limited degree in case. The only case adjectives have an explicit marker for is the accusative case - all other cases are zero-marked on adjectives.

Adjectives derived from nouns using the ending *-su* lose the final *-u* when taking the accusative ending. The *-u* is preserved when attaching the plural marker - but the plural marker's *ö* is dropped, leaving just the *-r*.

- *Zölarsu dapekai förasör* - The hairy boy sleeps
- *Fai litu zölarsi törénedi* - I see a hairy child
- *Zéten daigenai lék zölarsun* - The men are hairy

**Predicate Adjectives**
Predicate adjectives also agree with the noun they modify in number. Unlike attributive, prenominal adjectives, predicate adjectives also agree with animate subjects in the nominal case by using the nominal case ending *-ai*.
## Demonstratives
The two demonstratives are *zét* (animate) and *to* (inanimate). They agree with the nouns they modify in the same way regular adjectives do, matching both grammatical number and (only in the accusative) case.

When used as standalone pronouns, they decline for each of the following cases.


| Case          | Animate | Inanimate |
| ------------- | ------- | --------- |
| Nominative    | zétai   | to        |
| Accusative    | zéti    | ti        |
| Genitive      | zéto    | tés       |
| Oblique/other | zét     | to        |

| Case          | Animate Plural | Inanimate Plural |
| ------------- | -------------- | ---------------- |
| Nominative    | zétenai        | ten              |
| Accusative    | zéteni         | teni             |
| Genitive      | zéteno         | tens             |
| Oblique/other | zéten          | ten              |


- *Lit gai zéteni daigeni?* - Do you see those men?
- *Dulaide ti felaidi!* - Look at that tree!
- *Zétai kamennir biki fo* - That one took my spear
- *Ten kér fo* - I like those

While inanimate nouns do not take the nominative case ending, both as a determiner and as a pronoun, *to* uses the form *tai* which is a remnant of the historic inanimate accusative case.

- *To tok lékör tukait* - That rock is hard
## Personal Possession
C5-L1-A0 uses the genitive case pronouns to express personal possession. The genitive-case pronoun follows the noun being possessed.

- *Töréneden fo lék léseten* - My children are small
- *Mak dölöna végnir fé* - Your house burned down

Possessive pronouns are simply the personal pronoun in the genitive form, treated as a standalone nominal unit and declined as such. So in addition to the pronoun itself already being in the genitive case, it can receive the nominative, accusative, or genitive case endings like a regular noun.

- *To lékör go* - That one is yours
- *Fodai benéd udaitai* - Mine (animate) is growing tall
## Participial Adjectives
### Past Participle
The past participle is formed with the ending *-ner*, derived from the past tense ending *-nir*. The past participle is often used in predicate constructions to describe the resulting state of an event.

- *Ura lékör bégner* - It is full (filled)
- *Daigenai lék tökölaidnerenai* - The men are fallen
### Present Participle
The present participle is formed with the ending -le. The present participle's ending vowel is replaced by case endings when declined. The present participle cannot be used predicatively, only as a prenominal attributive adjective.

- *Börakle tok nebaidör vaikaif* - The rolling stone approaches quickly
- *Fai litu bugainli pelaidi* - I see the crawling animal
- *Gai lit bugainleni pelaideni* - You see the crawling animals
## Adverbs
Adverbs are formed simply by attaching *-aif* to the end of an adjective.

- *Mönai ranedaif fédenir berapi* - We slowly ate the chicken
- *Don bulaidaif tökölaidnir e bölat* - The sun fell redly in the sky

Participial adjectives can be changed in the same manner to form adverbs. In this case, the participial ending's vowel gets replaced by *-aif*.

- *Geladlaif urai fatnir dapeki uro e paned* - Lovingly he placed his boy in bed
- *Gai derégneraif pédenir* - You ran tiredly

Adverbs modifying an adjective or other adverb are placed directly before the modified word, and adverbs modifying a verb can be placed virtually anywhere within the sentence.
# Chapter - Prepositions
A variety of prepositions pair with nouns to create relational, locational, and directional links.

*Te* means "toward" and *böd* means "away from". These two prepositions compensate for the loss of an explicit ablative case and the loss of accusative case functioning as allative.

- *Fai tigu te e mak fo* - I go in to my house
- *Fai léku e mak fo* - I am inside my house
- *Fai tigu böd e mak fo* - I go from inside my house

Note that the last example is saying something all but unheard of in English - "going from inside" as opposed to "going outside".

Note however that the use of *böd* and *te* is rare outside of specific instances where disambiguation is necessary. The rest of the time, the main preposition combined with the verb carry the meaning on their own.

- *Fai tigu e mak fo* - I go in my house ("toward" direction implied)
- *Fai tigu sok mak* - I went out of the house ("away from" direction implied)

#### *I* - "In/Inside"
Denotes "in" or "inside".

- *Fai fatnir toki e gaset* - I placed the stone into the chest
- *Gai lék e mak* - You are inside the home
- *Mönai tignir e mak* - We went into the house

#### *Sok* - "Out"
Designates the location on the exterior or outside of something.

- *Fai bodenir sok mak* - I came out of the house
- *Mönai nekébnir dölöni sok mak* - We waited for you outside the  house
- *Gai lit sok mak* - You see out of the house 

#### *Uf* - "Nearby/At"
Denotes "at" or "in the vicinity of". Used for place names or general locations.

- *Tignir gai uf gelér?* - You went over to the girl
- *Urai fot uf mak* - He is standing near the home
- *Daigenai böd zenog zöponednir böd uf duraigen zökönaig* - The men traveled by canoe from the vicinity of the large settlement

#### *Kö* - "On"
Designates the location of the surface.

- *Lebét dölönai pedaiker kéleni kö zenog* - Will you all throw the fish onto the raft?
- *Urai pölainör kö tok* - He is sitting on the rock
- *Furail bafernir böd (kö) tok* - The bird hopped off of the rock

#### *Nönain* - "Under"
Literally an adjective meaning "low", *nönaina* used as a preposition denotes the location beneath something

- *Sipai bugainnir nönain paned* - The mouse crawled under the bed
- *Faid nönain férelen lékör hol* - The ground underneath the leaves is wet
#### *Udait* - "Above"
*Udait* means "at high elevation" when used as an adjective. Repurposed as a preposition it means "above".

- *Mönai pedaiknir udait gönal* - We threw stones over the hill
- *Daipai berulén udait bedék* - A crow flies above the village
- *Fomen hölaide böd udait zukulaiten* - The clouds drift in from above the mountains
#### *San* - "Between"
*San* literally means "center" or "middle". When *san* is used as a relational preposition, the nouns always take the genitive ending - literally formulating "in the middle of X and Y" where X and Y are genitive-marked.

- *Furailai berulénör san toks ö felaido* - The bird flies between a stone and a tree
- *Fai pölainu san fairelo ö sunolo* - I am sitting between Father and Mother
- *Dapekai bodenir böd san felaisuns makens* - The boy came out from among the wooden homes
#### Hök - "Behind"
*Hök* means "behind."

- *Fai fatu biki hök denai* - I placed the spear behind the door
- *Döragai pekeraisör hök felai* - The fox is hiding behind the tree
- *Hunai bodenir böd hök zenog* - They came out from behind the raft
#### *Beréd* - "In Front Of"
*Beréd* means "in front of".

- *Rutaigai bafernir kar beréd zukaid fo* - A salmon jumped up in front of my face
- *Mönai nekéb beréd mak* - We are waiting in front of the home
- *Urai bafernir böd beréd fedelaib* - He leapt from before the bear
#### *Ulaif* - "Around"
*Ulaif* means "around"..

- *Kurugainenai berulén ulaif gönal* - Eagles fly around the hill
#### *Tin* - "Across"
*Tin* comes from the verb *tin* which means "to cross". Unlike most prepositions, the noun following *tin* always takes the accusative case ending.

- *Fai dekelaiknir nibi tin toki* - I broke the branch across the stone
#### *Fö* - "About/For"
*Fö* means "about" or "for" depending on context.

- *Mönai zeterob fö urai* - We are speaking about him

When used to mean "on behalf of" or "for", the genitive case is used on the benefactor.

- *Hunai tainnir uri fö möna* - They did it for us

## Word Order
The default word order for sentences is as follows:
\[subject]\[verb]\[object]

While most adjectives and adverbs are tied to the position directly before the word they modify, adverbs and adverbial phrases modifying the verb of the sentence can be moved flexibly about to vary flow or emphasis.

- *Urai böd bik keroseknir deléberi* - He struck the deer with the spear
- *Dölönai dekelaknir zenogi neropneraif* - You completely broke the canoe

Extra information such as locative or temporal details can be placed either at the very beginning or very end of the sentence.
## Interrogative Particles
The interrogative particles are as follows.

| Lemma  | Gloss |
| ------ | ----- |
| kölai  | who   |
| ko     | what  |
| köfair | where |
| fökér  | why   |
| kék    | how   |
| ködon  | when  |
When used in questions they begin the sentence.

- *Ko léknir to?* - What is that?
- *Köfair mönai nét zéke?* - Where are we meeting?

*Kölai* and *ko* change form for the accusative case only: *köli* and *ki*.

- *Köli litnir gai?* - Who did you see?
## Relative Clauses
Relatives also use interrogative particles.

- *Fai litnir daigi, kölai kédenir faireli fo* - I saw the man who killed my father
- *Sönol nétnir dedelaidi, ki urai pötaiknir* - Mother found the peace she was looking for

Sometimes an idea can be either expressed with a relative clause OR with a participial adjective.

- *Daigai, kölai förasör, lékör duraigenai* - The man who is sleeping is big
- *Förasle daigai lékör duraigenai* - The sleeping man is big
# Chapter - Diachrony

**Sound changes**
**Stressed Vowel Shift**
- u -> o
- o -> a
- a -> ai
- e -> é
- i -> stays long i
**Unstressed Vowel Shift**
- u -> ö
- o -> ö
- a -> short u sound uhhhhh
- e -> e
- i (short i) -> e
**Stress on declined forms**
- stress remains on root primary syllable even when declined
- some high-frequency close-class forms, such as pronouns and interrogative particles, shifted stress to "case ending" syllable prior the vowel shift, so the root syllables are all unstressed.
