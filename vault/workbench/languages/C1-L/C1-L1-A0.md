# C1-L1-A0
[[culture-C1]]
# Overview
Maintains nounless predicate system from Proto. Introduces predicate declension and conjugation. This means one predicate "terfas" (be strong) can function as a verb (terfas\[1sg]) or as a noun (terfas\[nom/acc]) as it receives endings.
- prosody largely phased out, but initial glottal stop shifts from completive aspect -> past tense marker
- pre-existing roots as origins of endings? YES, sigi -> -si, while verb evolves to igi, so "igi terfassi" (rest on hard object)
**Conjugations**
- encode person, number, and referent newness for both subject and object of predicate
**Declensions**
- referential case endings
- encode referent type (affordance, characteristic behavior, actual current behavior, etc) and syntactic role (grammatical case) in a single ending
**Modifiers**
- predicates can be used as verbal and nominal modifiers
- nominally, preceding predicates declined in same way as object or subject modify what follows
- verbally, preceding predicates conjugated in same way as main predicate modify what follows
**Poetry/prose**
- everyday speech is already halfway to poetry
- instead of having to choose between tree, oak, pine, towering giant, they can choose between shade-giver, fruit-grower, root-holder, wood-source, nesting-place
- Poetry probably sounds relatively surprisingly plain to speakers since it is less "unusual" than English poetry with its metaphors

# Chapter 1 - Overview & Typology
This language is descended from one that had no nouns and no nominalization. This language has evolved to the environment of its speakers and now has several mechanisms that introduce true nominalization of the language's predicate roots. Lexical nouns have not solidified.

The main concept revolves around a single type of root word (predicates) that can be either conjugated to express an action, event, process, or ongoing state, or they can be declined to denote a referent to something in the shared context of the speakers. Referents are selected dynamically real-time, most commonly opting to identify the most salient quality, behavior, or characteristic of the thing being referred to.

The worldview of speakers is predicate-centric, and this is reflected in the way they compose sentences and express ideas.

# Chapter 2 - Phonology
**Note on written language:** C1-L1 in A0 did not yet have a written form, therefore, in this grammar, we will be using roman alphabet approximations to represent the spoken language.

The phonology is represented by the following characters:
- l - regular l like in English "lick"
- r -lightly trilled Hungarian-style r
- b - voiced bilabial as in English "boat"
- p - voiceless bilabial as in English "octopus"
- f - voiceless labiodental fricative as in English "friend"
- v - voiced labiodental fricative as in English "victory"
- d - voiced dental fricative as in English "there"
- t - voiceless alveolar plosive as in English "tip"
- s - voiceless alveolar fricative like English "same"
- sh - voiceless postalveolar fricative like English "share"
- w - voiced labial-velar approximant like English "owing"
- y - voiced palatal approximant like English "young"
- j - voiced palatal lateral approximant like Spanish "pollo", or something between English "yet" and "jet" (but simply "y" in English "yet" when not followed by a vowel)
- gy - voiced palatal plosive like Hungarian "gyerek"
- n - voiced uvular nasal like English "nope"

**Vowels**
- a - open-back unrounded vowel, like English "pause"
- o - close-mid back rounded vowel, closest in English is "go" (but no dipthong)
- u - close back rounded vowel, like French "vous"
- i - close front unrounded vowel, like English "seat"
- e - open-mid front unrounded vowel, like English "set"
- ö - close-mid front rounded vowel like German "schön"
- ü - close front rounded vowel like German "tür"

**A Note on Stem Alternations**
Historically, C1 underwent a sound shift whereby _g_ became _y_ before front vowels and _l_ became _w_ before back vowels. These alternations remain productive in the modern language and apply across morphological boundaries, including predicate conjugation and declension.

For the purposes of this rule, the front vowels are _i, e, ö,_ and _ü_, while the back vowels are _a, o,_ and _u_.

In practice this only matters for the *g*-*y* alternation as no predicates end in the letter *l*.

Examples:

- *fojag* (to be good) - *fojayese* (goodness)
- *dojog* (to roll)- *dojoyö* (you roll)
# Chapter 3 - Predicate Structure
## Predicate Theory
All lexical roots are fundamentally predicative. They encode events, states, affordances, or relations. There are no distinct grammatical classes such as verbs, nouns, or adjectives - rather, all roots fall under one predicative category by default.

Historically predicate roots resisted nominalization. This language has evolved with case endings that nominalize predicate roots, effectively rendering them noun-like for specific grammatical and syntactic roles.

Any time a predicate receives a case ending, it becomes nominal, effectively "thing that does or affords X" where X is the predicate root.

In spite of grammatical nominalization, there are no nominal lexical items.

For example, *sarenogon* means "shade-give". When nominalized with a case ending such as *sarenogon\[case]* it might be used to refer to a tree, or to a bush, or two a tent awning, depending on context. The most salient objects and concepts often have "characteristic" referent predicates, which means in general, *sarenogon\[case]* is understood to refer to trees absent a more disambiguating shared context. 

When used nominally, predicates may denote:
- characteristic behavior (characteristic reference, the closest thing to noun labels)
- current activity
- affordance
- environmental relation
- bodily state
- perceptual event
**They are only nominal when declined using case endings.**
## Reference System
Without lexical nouns, C1 relies on a reference system to identify "things" using the predicate roots. Things and objects in the world do not have properly fixed "labels" in the way most languages tend to do. Rather referent predicates are selected based on factors such as characteristic behavior, salient current behavior, or affordance offered by the object or thing in question.
## Characteristic Reference
The closest thing to a "fixed label". Predicates selected for characteristic reference answer the question, "What does it do characteristically? What enduringly defines it?"

- *sarenogoniy* - shade-giver (tree)
- *faravasiy* - swimmer (fish)
- *fatasiy* - leaper (rabbit)
## Situational Reference
Predicates in this method answer, "What is it doing *right now?*" They focus on the current salient behavior, state, or action of the thing being referred to.

Two people watch a spider leap between two parts of a tree.

- *fatasiy* - leaper (now spider, because that is the currently most salient leaper in the shared context)

Someone sees a friend swimming up to their boat from the shore.

- *faravasiy* - swimmer (now friend who is currently swimming)

Situational reference use predicates to dynamically refer to something in the context based on what it is currently doing.
## Relational Reference
Relational reference identifies something based on its relationship to something else. A single thing or individual is identified differently from different relational perspectives

To the partner of the person:
- *gyavaniy* - bonder (partner/spouse)

To the child:
- *bamasiy* - sire-er (father), or
- *sövaniy* - birth-er (mother)

English speakers do this already with the same kinds of kinship terms.

To the traveling companion:
- *neresüriy* - traveler

To the village elder:
- *niwpando* - grower-from-small (young one)
# Chapter 4 - Predicate Conjugation
C1 adapted to use predicate endings as a way of encoding information previously left to shared context and once-prevalent prosody overlays. This chapter will cover one group of such endings called predicate conjugation. As the second word implies, these endings function similar to verb endings in other languages.

More specifically, predicate conjugations encode grammatical person and number, as well as the newness or knownness of both the subject and object of said predicate in the discourse context. This newness/knownness is referred to as the **discourse status** of a subject or object. Predicate conjugations do not encode tense or aspect, although another mechanism exists for expressing completed past actions, to be discussed at the end of this chapter.

There are two main types of predicate conjugation - endings that track a single participant (intransitive) and those that track two participants (transitive). Due to combinatorial explosion transitive conjugations are by far the more complex of the two. Intransitive conjugation will be discussed first, with transitive conjugation following.
## Participant Tracking
As mentioned previously, predicate conjugations track the subject (and object if present) and whether or not each is being newly introduced or referencing an already known thing. This known-new distinction only applies where third person participants are present. First and second person participants lack this distinction.

## Speech Conventions
### Lack of Pronouns
Since C1 has no pronouns, speakers rely on conjugation endings to encode who plays what role. When they wish to emphasize a specific thing or person, an explicit referent is used (the person's name, a situational referent describing their current action or state, etc).

Speaking to Garn:
- *Durösata gapawas-pütoniyar* - I give you(implied) a spear
- *Durösata gapawas-pütoniyar Garniyü* - I give you(explicit) a spear

### Overt Participant Identification
When identifying multiple first person participants, the speaker may omit themselves or overtly identify themselves based on discourse need and ambiguity risk.

Belar speaking:
- *Nataniy gyoyüali terfas-yejaniyü* - Natan and I are going to the hard-slope (cliff/mountain)
- *Nataniy go Belariy gyoyüali terfas-yejaniyü* - Natan and Belar are going to the hard-slope (cliff/mountain)

In the first example above, the plurality of the first person conjugation denotes the speaker's participation in the activity.
### Plurality
Since there are no nouns and therefore no plural nouns, C1 encodes plurality of participants and undergoers in the above conjugation endings.

- *Teraniy neresürna'a* - My sibling travels
- *Teraniy neresürni'a* - The siblings travel

Alternatively speakers use the collective referent type to specify a collective group. The convention is to use singular conjugation with the collective, but if the individuals of the group are to be emphasized then the plural conjugation maybe be employed.

- *Terannad neresürna'a* - The group of siblings travels
- *Terannad neresürni'a* - The group of siblings travel
## Past-completive
A relic of older prosody overlays, the past-completive marker is a glottal stop at the onset of the conjugated predicate. It is marked with a colon here ":". Speakers use it to denote actions that occurred in the past, although it is frequently omitted from casual interactions where context or additional time-marking expressions disambiguate.

- *:Yüd ternesiyan* - I slept in the shelter
- *Yüd ternesiyan :gosuw* - I slept in the shelter before now ("time-passed")
# Chapter 5 - Predicate Declension
## Predicate Declension Overview
Predicate conjugation tracks participants in an event.

Predicate declension allows predicates themselves to function as referential expressions (the participants in the event).

This is important because C1 has no lexical nouns (tree, house, water, etc.) Instead it uses the declension system to nominalize the verb-like predicates "real-time" in a sentence.

Declension in C1 combines **referent type** and **grammatical case** into a single predicate ending. Both of these concepts will be covered in the first half of this chapter, with an overview of the actual predicate endings and declension tables in the second half.
## Referent Types
In C1, case morphology does not merely mark syntactic role; it also determines which facet of a predicate is being treated as a referent.

In English we have verbs like "hunt". We then can use derivational morphology or additional words to arrive at a noun version of said verb, or sometimes the verb itself doubles as a noun. "Hunt" becomes "hunter" or "hunting ground". C1's referential case endings fill this exact role, only they do not form dictionary "nouns" that label a specific object, but rather modify predicates "on-the-fly" mid-sentence to change them into a referent. Just like how English has different potential noun outcomes from a single verb, C1 has several referent types to choose from when declining a predicate.
### Participant Referent
The participant referent is one of the most useful. It refers to something characterized by the predicate. 

- *Virpon* (to hunt) - *virponiy* (one who hunts, hunter).
### Undergoer Referent
The undergoer referent refers to something that undergoes or is the target of the action described by the predicate.

- *Virpon* (to hunt) - *virpono* (one who is hunted, prey)
### Concept Referent
The concept referent is slightly broader semantically. It refers to event or process itself that the predicate describes, or it can also refer to abstract ideas (like goodness, hope, sadness).

- *Virpon* (to hunt) - *virponese* (activity of hunting, "the hunt")
- *Fojag* (to be good) - *fojayese* (goodness, wellness)
### Result Referent
The result referent primarily refers to the outcome or product of a predicate action or process. Additionally it can sometimes denote a goal or objective of the predicate action.

- *Sagun* (to build/craft) - *sagunwa* (construction, built thing)
- *Neresür* (to walk, journey/travel) - *neresürwa* (destination)

Note that something like "the journey" could be expressed using the previous concept referent ending:

- *Neresür* - *neresürese*
### Affordance Referent
The affordance referent, or instrumental referent, uses the predicate to describe what the thing in question affords or offers.

- *Tirand* (to cut) - *tirandaf* (cutting tool, knife)
- *Sarenogon* (to give shade) - *sarenogonaf* (tree, lean-to, or possibly clouds)
### Location Referent
The location referent refers to something where the predicate action or process takes place.

- *Virpon* (to hunt) - *virponvor* (hunting grounds)
- *Sövan* (to give birth) - *sövanvor* (birthplace)
### Collective Referent
The collective referent is one of the more defining aspects of the language. It refers to groups of people who are characterized by the predicate. Since there are no lexical nouns, C1 has no plural endings, and collective referents play a critical part in filling this role.

- *Pajan* (to be familiar) - *pajannad* (community)
- *Virpon* (to hunt) - *virponnad* (hunting party)
- *Niwpand* (to grow big from small) - *niwpandnad* (the group of children)

Unlike equivalent expressions in English, this type of construct is highly productive in C1 and can be used with virtually any predicate.

- *Fojag* (to be good) - *fojagnad* (the group of good ones)
- *Yepnas* (to float-hold) - *yepnasnad* (the group of float-holders/boats)

Since the collective referent implies a group of referents, and the type of referents in the group is not overtly specified (i.e. participant vs. undergoer, etc.), collective referents simply refer to collective entity of referents of whatever type is most salient or intuitive based on context - hence *virponiy* -> *virponnad* but *niwpando* -> *niwpandnad*.

**Note**
When conjugating a predicate performed by a collective referent, standard practice is to conjugate in the singular rather than the plural. However, when emphasizing the individuals in the collective group, a plural conjugation may be used.

## Grammatical Cases
Grammatical case is the second information encoded in predicate declension endings. Grammatical case denotes the syntactic role of a word in a sentence. Each of the grammatical cases are covered below.

Eight of the ten grammatical cases are locative cases and originated from old locative-encoding predicate root families. Although they each continue to retain their original spatial meanings, several have acquired additional grammatical functions. The most notable instances of this are overtly labeled with both the locative meaning and syntactic one, i.e. the through/accusative case.

It is worth noting that in the previous section covering referent types, each of the examples was given in the **nominative case**.
### Nominative
The nominative case denotes the subject of the sentence.

- *Virponiy neresürna* - A hunter walks
- *Faravasiy pelyendna'a* - The fish resists the current
### Through/Accusative
The through/accusative case historically simply served as a locative case meaning "through", but now fills a prominent secondary role marking the object in a sentence.

- *:Neresürni'a sarenogonnadar*
- *Segandnoja faravasiyar yesiy* - The fisherman pulls in a fish
### Near/Dative
The near/dative case originally meant "near" but now doubles as the dative case, as seen in ditransitive constructions or when denoting the destination of a movement action. It can also mark beneficiaries of an action.

- *:Durösaji segand-yesafar niwpandyü* - We gave the fishing line to the child
- *Gyoyüni'a vöyüvoyü* - They are going to the gathering place
- *:Sagajatar fatasiyar Tufesiyü* - I killed the rabbits for Tufes
### Above/Benefactive
The above/benefactive case, while less productive than the ones listed above, has a couple of key uses: marking the location above the declined referent, and marking the beneficiaries of an action. This is different than the Near/Dative's benefactive, as it has more of a sense of "on behalf of" or "for the cause of".

- *:Garenna'a terenesiye* - He glanced above the shelter
- *:Teljerna'a terannye* - He/she died for his/her family
### Inside
The inside case denotes the location inside the referent it attaches to.

- *Buyini'o niwpandor yüdvan* - They place the child into the bed
### Outside
The outside case denotes the location outside the referent it attaches to.
### On
The on case denotes the location on the referent it attaches to.
### Under
The under case denotes the location under the referent it attaches to.
### Across
The across case denotes the space and direction across the referent it attaches to.
### From
The from case denotes the direction coming from the referent it attaches to. It is also used to express the means by which an action is accomplish, in an instrumental sense.

- *:Sagajataj fatasiyar gapawas-pütoniyde* - I killed the rabbit with a spear
## Choosing Referential Case Endings
Now that we have covered the two axes individually (referent type and grammatical case) we will now demonstrate how the two come together when selecting a referential case ending appropriate for a given situation.

| from              | iyde        | de        | ede     | le           | ade                     | vode     | ne         |

A helpful way to think about it is by first asking what object you are identifying, and what aspect of that object is being described by the predicate you chose. This is what dictates the referent type.

If the predicate action is being performed by the object, then the participant type is likely the correct choice. If the predicate action itself is the thing you wish to reference, the concept type is more fitting. And so forth.

With the referent type selected you can now decide what syntactic or relational role the referent will play in your sentence. Is it the subject? -> Nominative. Is it a location inside? -> Inside. Is it the source of an action? -> From.

This cognitive selection process is central to native speakers' worldview and cognition. The world is defined in terms of what is happening, what is being done - not fixed labels.

- *Noryeledna virponvoye yelneriy* - A bird glided above the hunting ground
- *Garennoja hashewuiyar waböreniyan virponiy* - The hunter spotted something thrashing in the rippling water

Learners and young children frequently produce constructions in which the predicate and the referent are derived from the same root:

- *Yelnerna'a yelneriy* - The flyer flies

While grammatical, such constructions are often perceived as redundant by fluent speakers, who generally omit the referent or select a different predicate to describe the action in a different way.

- *Yelnerna'a* - Flying is happening / it flies
- *Noryeledna'a yelneriy* - The flyer glides
# Chapter 6 - Predicate Modifiers & Comparison
## Modifer Syntax & Agreement
In C1, predicates can modify predicates and referents can modify referents. Any root used as a modifier must be conjugated or declined to match the conjugation or declension of the root it modifies, and precedes the modified root directly. If the glottal stop onset is used to express an action that happened in the past, modifier roots must also have the glottal stop.

The following example show conjugated predicates can be modified:

- *:Nötenna'a :yeyena'a segand-yesnad* - The group of fishermen rose quickly

The following example show the modification of declined referents:

- *:Garenatya nöteniyar yelneriyar sarenogonnye* - We saw a fast bird/flyer above the forest
## Comparisons
Historically, prosody features such as interrupting glottal stops were used to vary the intensity of predicate roots, i.e. *fatas* vs. *fa'atas*. While this feature has faded as a productive tool of expression, it has remained lexicalized in certain roots, and one in particular serves as an intensifying modifier: *bu'urö*, or literally "very big/bigger".

The intensifying modifier can be used simply as an intensifier ("very") or as a comparative/superlative modifier. When used as a comparative modifier it pairs with the negative particle *gya* to contrast against the thing being compared to.

Intensifier examples:

- *Bu'urö'aw* - I am very big
- *Bu'urö'aw terfasaw* - I am very strong (I am very big strong)
- *Bu'uröna'a gyatonna'a* - He runs a lot

Comparison examples:

- *Bu'uröna'a gyatonna'a gya Tufes* - He runs more than Tufes
- *Bu'urö'ö liserö gya Garn* - You talk more than Garn
- *Bu'uröna'a gyaresna'a gyoyüna'a burö-sandwan gya Gelner* - He goes into the cave more frequently than Gelner
- *Bu'urö'aw nötenaw* - I am very fast / I am the fastest (implied superlative based on context)
# Chapter 7 - Clause Types & Referential Constructions
## Clause Types & Syntax
There are two high-level clause types in C1. Event clauses and referential clauses. Event clauses are largely all that has been shown until this point in the grammar. They describe an action or event. Referential clauses fulfill many of the functions served by relative clauses and possessive constructions in English, and these will be the primary focus of this chapter.

While generally speaking, C1 has free word order, there is a conventional order used for event clauses, and a mandatory word order for referential clauses.

### Event Clauses
The word order for event clauses defaults to:

\[predicate]\[object]\[additional info]\[subject]

The key here is that the conjugated predicate fronts the entire thing, as the even or action occurring is the most relevant information to the speakers. The subject, being naturally encoded in the conjugation ending, is most dispensable and is only ever overtly stated when context fails to disambiguate.

- *Yeyenojo niwpanor terfas-yejandiyi bamasiy* - The father lifted his child up the rocky slope
- *:Garenöy faravasiyar? Derenasna'a* - Did you see a fish? It drift-floats (presumably down a river)

### Referential Clauses
Referential clauses are a way of packing more information into a referent than what a single predicate would allow. In English translation these usually come out as relative clauses or possessive constructions. The referential clause word order is as follows.

**Object Modified**
\[subject]\[predicate]\[any additional information]\[object]

**Subject Modified**
\[predicate]\[any additional information]\[subject]

**Subject Modified, No Additional Predicate**
\[additional information]\[subject]\

The core referent being modified by the clause is the subject or object that comes at the very end.

It is crucial to note that **the modified referent is declined according to its grammatical role in the greater expression, not its role in the referential clause** - hence the restriction on referential clause word order.

To clarify, here are some examples.

- *:Savatöna Bamasiy sagajatnojo fatasiyar* - You at the rabbit Father killed
- *Siyina yeshetayi Bamasiy sagajatnojo fatasiy* - The rabbit Father killed hangs on the drying rack

Note that in the first example, *fatasiyar* is in the accusative case not because Father killed it, but because you ate it - and in the second example *fatasiy* is in the nominative since it is doing the hanging from the drying rack.

Also worth noting is the fact that conjugated predicates in referential clauses do not typically receive the past/completive glottal stop onset unless context leaves meaning ambiguous.

Contrast between default event clause order (first example) and referential clause order (second example):

- *Garenaj virponiyar puwsaniyi yeshetayü* - I see the hunter by the drying rack on the river-bank (emerge-from-water-er)
- *Garenaj puwsaniyi yeshetayü virponiyar* - I see the hunter by the drying rack on the river-bank (emerge-from-water-er)

When the final referent of a referential clause is omitted, the final predicate of the clause becomes the head referent and receives the appropriate case marking:

- *Garenaj Bamasiy sagajatnojo'iyar* - I see the one that Father killed

Further examples:

- *:Savataji wurapna'a sandwan ternesiyde sopwariyar* - We drank the water that dripped from the shelter and pooled in the depression
- *:Siyina'a peneriyi virponiy sagajatnojo fatasiy* - The rabbit that the hunter killed remained on the ground
- *Yeshetayü siyi'awiy garenaj virponiyar puwsaniyde* - Standing next to the drying rack, I saw the hunter from the river bank

Note that sometimes an event clause and referential clause appear identical on the surface, but the referential clause belongs in a greater sentence with an additional conjugated predicate:

- *Wurapna sandwan ternesiyde sopwariy* - Water dripping from the shelter pools in the depression/hollow
- *Siyina'a puwsaniyi yeshetayü virponiy* - The hunter is next to the drying rack by the river bank
- *Sawnaranna yelneriyar siyina'a puwsaniyi yeshetayü virponiy* - The hunter next to the drying rack by the river bank watches the flyer
## Relational Expressions
C1 does not have a grammatical possessive construct or possessive particles, rather it uses predicates to express specific relationships, most commonly *pajan* (familiar-be), *pa'ajan* (close-friend-be), *teran* (blood-share), and *vewas* (responsible-for-be). The relational predicates are conjugated to agree with the participants involved, and the relational construct follows the **referential clause** pattern, with the described referent's case selected to fit its grammatical role in the greater expression

- *Belariy terannojo niwpando* - Belar's child
- *:Garenaj Belariy terannoja niwpandor* - I saw one of Belar's children
- *Vewasaj segand-yesaf* - The fishing line I am responsible for
- *Durösaj vewasaj segand-yesafar Gapalasiyü* - I give my fishing line to Gapalas

Notice in the last example it almost appears *durösaj vewasaj* is a modifier relationship with *durösaj* modifying *vewasaj*, even though in reality *vewasaj* is the beginning of the relational referential clause. While this can cause ambiguity in some instances, usually semantics help clarify (how would "to give" modify "to be responsible for" semantically?) and, worst case scenario, the speaker could insert their own name at the beginning of the referential clause like thus:

- *Durösaj Garn vewasaj segand-yesafar Gapalasiyü* - I give my (Garn's) fishing line to Gapalas
# Chapter 8 - Word Order & Focus
## Default Word Order
Due to the extensive case marking and conjugation systems, word order in C1 is technically free. However, speakers prefer a default order for neutral statements, which frees up non-standard word orders to highlight important information.

As touched on briefly in the chapter on clause types, the default word order for an event clause is as follows:

\[predicate]\[object]\[additional info]\[subject]

The main conjugated predicate fronts the entire phrase, being by default the most important piece of information contained in a sentence. The overt subject can be left until the very end or omitted entirely, as it is always encoded in the main conjugated predicate.
## Focus Position
The focus position is at the very beginning of a sentence. By default the main predicate occupies this space, but virtually any part of a sentence can be relocated here to emphasize its importance, or when making contrastive comparisons. Take a look at the following default sentence followed by multiple reinterpretations based on what is being emphasized.

- *Buyinojo bawaswar yüdayi Bamasiy* - Father placed the fruit on the sleeping mat
- *Bamasiy buyinojo bawaswar yüdayi* - *Father* placed the fruit on the sleeping mat
- *Yüdayi buyinojo bawaswar Bamasiy* - Father placed the fruit on *the sleeping mat*
- *Bawaswar buyinojo yüdayi Bamasiy* - Father placed *the fruit* on the sleeping mat
- *Bawaswar yüdayi buyinojo Bamasiy* - Father placed *the fruit on the sleeping mat*

As you can see from the last example, you can put multiple words in the focus position if you so desire.

## Questions
Binary questions are formed with the same word order as their declarative counterparts. The only thing that changes is an upward intonation *solely on the word the question is about*. This in contrast to English question intonation, where the entire sentence goes from low to high. In this grammar upward intonation on words is marked with "/".

- *Galernow/ Bamasiy* - Does Father love me?
- *:Fatasö/ gapawas-terfasiyde* - You jumped from the tall rock?

Questions interact with the focus position very intuitively.

- *Bamasiy/ galernow* - Does *Father* love me?
- *Gapawas-terfasiyde/ :fatasö* - You jumped from *the tall rock?*

For open-ended questions the interrogative particle can be used (see the chapter on interrogatives for more detail). Questions with an interrogative particle have the particle in the focus position at the beginning of the phrase.

- *Yiy/ sagajatnajo Bamasiyar* - Who killed Father?
- *Yiye/ desalö* - Why did you leave me?
# Chapter 9 - Interrogatives, Particles, & Negation
## Interrogatives
C1 inherited a single uncertainty particle *yi*. On its own it was underspecified, but C1 has adapted and uses declined forms to ask specific questions. The following table illustrates the main options as derived from the ten primary grammatical cases

| Interrogative                    | particle |
|----------------------------------|----------|
| what nom                         | yiy      |
| inside what                      | yiyan    |
| outside what                     | yiyon    |
| on what, "where"                 | yiyi     |
| under what                       | yiyu     |
| above what, for what purpose/why | yiye     |
| near what, for whom/what         | yiyü     |
| through what, who/what acc       | yiyar    |
| across what                      | yiyö     |
| from what                        | yiyde    |

Additionally, questions like "When?" can be expressed by combining the particle *yi* with a time-passage predicate such as *gosuw*, *gyogas*, or *sertand* *(yigosuw, yigyogas, yisertand)*. These essentially ask the questions "How much time?", "How many days?", and "How many moon cycles?".

- *Yiyü/ yelnerni'a* - Where are they flying?
- *:Yisertand/ yeferöye* - How many moon cycles ago did you arrive?
- *Yiyde/ :neresürna'a* - Where did he travel from?
## Particles
**Non-factual/Interrogative Particle**
The *yi* particle also serves an additional purpose.

- *Yi/ teyü'ata savatafar, savataw* - if I find food, I will eat

**Coordinating Particle**
The particle *go* comes from the word *gogon* (add-to) and is used to connected related referents as a group.

- *Fatasiy go faravasiy sobenniy gosüdniy* - Rabbits and fish breathe differently

**Contrastive/Emphatic Particle**
The particle *ni* means "but" or "however". It can also be used as an emphatic interjection at the end of phrases.

- *:Vapunaw gyoyü lerandiyi, ni :sogadna'a yepnasiy* - I wanted to go out on the water, but the boat sank
- *Durösöna, ni!* - Give it to me!
## Negation
Originally the only negation-adjacent root was *gyawupon* (originally *jalupon*). Its literal translation is "to try but fail". This root persists and retains its original literal meaning.

- *:Gyawuponaw wapun gyoyü* - I tried but failed to want to go (or more accurately, me wanting to go tried but failed to come to pass)

This root filled the semantic space of a proper negative particle, and overtime, when used in those contexts, it reduced to a simple *gya.* Keep in mind that *gya* did not replace *gyawupon*, but was adopted as an additional particle word to serve as a dedicated negator, among other functions (see the section on comparisons in the chapter on modifiers).

- *Gya wapunaw gyoyü* - I do not want to go
- *Gya :gabunna'a Gojö* - Gojö did not survive

Before the prosodic distinctions fell out of use, *gya'a* was formed as more absolute counterpart to *gya*, most often translated as "never".

- *Gya'a fömaröna segand-yes* - You never show (me) how to fish

To express an idea like "nobody/nothing does X", speakers simply negate an unconjugated predicate without an explicit subject, i.e. "X does not occur." 

- *Gya liser* - Talking does not
- *Gya garen* - Seeing does not

Speakers do not generally distinguish between "nobody does X" and "X does nothing" - both are simply expressed as the predicate in negated form. However, when more precision is desired, they use negative forms of the interrogative particle as a negative indefinite particle.

- *Gya garenaw gyayiyar* - I do not see anything

Certain negated interrogative particles have specialized use cases.

- *:Neresürali sortvoyü gyayiye* - We traveled here in vain/for nothing (lit. above nothing)
# Chapter 10- Occurrence Predicates (Numbers)
## Occurrence Roots
Instead of a regular numbering system, C1 counts *occurrences* with specially adapted predicates. Each of the five predicates evolved from another predicate with which the given number is associated in some way.

| Lemma   | Gloss        |

| gyavas  | occur-twice  |
| vovewas | occur-thrice |
| waras   | occur-fource |
| bewas   | occur-fice   |
Typically speakers count occurrences up to five on one hand. When counting past five, they track full sets of five on their second hand while tracking ones on the first hand, all the way up to 30. ##  Usage
Occurrence predicates are used like regular modifier predicates (refer to chapter on modifiers). When modifying another predicate, they conjugate or decline to match the conjugation or declension of said predicate.

- *:Garenar warasiyar virponiyar* - I saw four hunters (literally four-times-occur-ers hunters)
- *:Bewasar :sövanar niwpandor* - I gave birth five times to a child (I gave birth to five children)

Note that in the second example, the natural tendency is to apply the counting predicate to the action and not the object, whenever semantically reasonable.

- *Bojarniy vovewaso niwpando* - Three children are playing
- *:Gyavasöy :piranöy segand-yesayar* - You lost a fishing line twice
# Chapter 11 - Diachrony
Coming from C4-C1 Proto language baseline:
- g becomes y before front vowels and rounded vowels, stays g or shifts to voiced velar fricative before back vowels
- j becomes gy word initially, like spanish ll elsewhere
- l becomes w before back vowels and consonants and at word-final positions, stays l before front vowels
- rn clusters disfavored, therefore e inserted between the two sounds "ren"
- d -> th (English "the")

**Future notes**
- in later ages, when nouns become lexicalized, they will likely all share the same ending - a remnant of the nominative case ending used on verbal predicates in A0
- different lexical nouns will likely have different referent-type variants of the nominative case
- eventually people will not recognize the history, simply notice, "huh, all nouns seem to end in this letter, but no one knows why"
